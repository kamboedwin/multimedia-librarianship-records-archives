LIU08106 / RIU08106 · Unit 05

Video, Animation and Multimedia Storytelling

Plan, capture, edit, caption and preserve responsible moving-image information resources.

Start here: a familiar situation

A new student needs to learn how to search an online catalogue. A short video can show the cursor moving through the actual steps, while captions allow the instructions to be followed without sound. Before recording, the creator decides what the student should learn and what each scene should show. That plan becomes a storyboard. A records office could use the same process to explain a records-request procedure.

Key words in simple language

  • Frame: a single image within moving video.
  • Frame rate: how many frames are displayed each second.
  • Storyboard: a scene-by-scene visual plan of the message.
  • Caption: synchronised text representing speech and important sounds.

Think about it: When would a short illustrated guide work better than a video? The sections below explain how to answer this question.

For other terms, see the course multimedia glossary.

Learning pathway: Define the purpose → plan a story → record or create visuals and sound → edit a working copy → caption and describe → quality-check → document and publish. The exercises use simulated data or your own authorised recordings.

5.1 Learning outcomes

By the end of this unit, you should be able to explain how digital video and animation work; interpret frame rate, pixel dimensions, aspect ratio, bitrate, codec and container properties; design a storyboard and shot list; record stable and legible video; edit a simple sequence; create and verify caption cues; prepare an accessible video and descriptive transcript; distinguish source, project and access files; and evaluate the quality, ethics and usability of a finished resource.

The practical project brings these skills together in a 60–90 second professional video for librarianship or records and archives management. A responsible professional result is judged by its purpose, production process, documented evidence and usability—not only the appearance of the final MP4.

5.2 What is video and why use it?

Video is a sequence of visual frames shown over time, often synchronised with recorded or designed sound. Each frame can be understood as a still digital image. When displayed at suitable intervals, successive images convey movement, change and the order in which actions take place. A typical video can therefore contain moving images, speech, titles, sound effects, animation and other media elements.

A library may use video to demonstrate how a learner searches the catalogue, renews a loan, uses an online repository or accesses a specialised service. The user needs to see the action while hearing or reading an explanation. A records office or archive may show an approved procedure for preparing records for transfer, document a collection's context, interpret an exhibition or train staff in safe handling. Here, accuracy, provenance and security are especially important. An edited demonstration is not necessarily an unaltered evidential recording.

Video is not always the best medium. A short text checklist may be faster to scan, easier to print and cheaper to access on a low-bandwidth connection. Choose video when motion, sequence, interaction, human demonstration or temporal context genuinely improve understanding. Offer a suitable alternative for people who cannot watch or hear it.

5.3 A purposeful production workflow

Video production is usually divided into three overlapping stages. Pre-production clarifies the audience, learning or communication objective, message, duration, script, permissions, storyboard, shot list, sound and equipment. Production captures video, photographs, screen footage and sound, with quality checks and basic file documentation. Post-production arranges the footage, trims and corrects it, adds narration and titles, prepares captions and descriptions, exports an access copy, and tests the result.

The sequence can be represented as follows:

Audience need → Objective → Script/storyboard → Capture → Edit → Caption/describe → Test → Release and retain

Do not begin by asking which transition effect looks interesting. First state what a viewer should be able to know, understand or do. For example, “After viewing this ninety-second library video, a new student can locate an available e-book using the catalogue.” That objective is more useful than “Create an attractive library promotional video.” It tells you what must be shown, what words must be explained and how to evaluate success.

Applied planning examples

Programme Audience and need Video objective Useful evidence
BLISS New students unfamiliar with the online catalogue Demonstrate a title search and how to interpret availability Screen capture, clear narration, test with a new student
BRIM Records assistants learning a controlled transfer procedure Demonstrate a fictional, approved handover workflow Labelled demonstration, authorisation check, edit log and version record

When filming people or handling existing media, verify permission for recording and the specific intended use. Consent to record an interview or a meeting does not automatically authorise unrestricted online publication. Avoid real confidential records in student exercises; use the synthetic practice resource where possible.

5.4 Script, storyboard and shot list

A script is the planned message: narration, dialogue, key on-screen text, sound cues and important visible actions. A storyboard describes the visual sequence in separate panels. Each panel normally contains an approximate scene duration, a description or sketch of the view, the speaker's words, any on-screen labels and the reason the scene is needed. A shot list turns the storyboard into a practical recording checklist, including camera position, framing, props, location, permissions and audio requirements.

Suppose you want to build an introductory catalogue video. A simple storyboard could be:

Scene Time What the viewer sees What the viewer hears/reads Purpose
1 0–6 s Intro title and library service name “Find a resource in the library catalogue.” State the task
2 6–12 s Catalogue search bar “Enter a title or subject in the search box.” Show first action
3 12–18 s Fictional results display “Check the title, location and availability.” Explain interpretation
4 18–24 s Next action and help channel “Open the record for access details.” Make the learning actionable

Our original downloadable synthetic video follows this four-scene pattern. It contains no actual catalogue account, personal information or confidential archive record. You can play it in the interactive studio and inspect its accompanying WebVTT captions.

Scriptwriting principles

Write for spoken understanding: keep sentences direct; define unfamiliar terms; introduce each screen action before or while it occurs; and let the learner see the result. Read the script aloud to estimate duration. A script is not automatically accessible: crucial onscreen information must also be communicated to users who cannot see the video.

5.5 Digital video: frames, frame rate and duration

A frame is a still picture that belongs to a video sequence. Frame rate, measured in frames per second (fps), describes how frequently frames are shown. For constant-frame-rate video, approximate frame count equals duration multiplied by frame rate. A ten-second clip at 25 fps has approximately 10 × 25 = 250 frames. At 30 fps, the same duration has about 300 frames.

Frame rate does not tell you how quickly a clip is played if the playback speed changes, and some phone or screen recordings use variable frame rate. Standard editing applications can display or convert such material to a project rate, but the converted output should be checked for timing or audio-sync problems.

There is no universally best frame rate. About 24 fps is often used for a film-like appearance; 25 or 30 fps can suit general explanatory videos; 50 or 60 fps may help with rapid motion or demonstrations that need smoother tracking. Higher frame rates can require additional storage, processing and bandwidth at similar per-frame quality. For a largely static catalogue tutorial, clear legibility matters more than maximising fps.

Worked example

A 90-second instruction video at 30 fps contains approximately 90 × 30 = 2,700 frames. If it is exported at 15 fps, it contains about 1,350 frames. This calculation does not predict the exact file size, because compression, content complexity and bitrate also matter.

5.6 Resolution, aspect ratio and framing

Video resolution identifies the pixel dimensions of each frame. A 1920 × 1080 frame contains 2,073,600 pixels (about 2.07 megapixels). A 1280 × 720 frame has 921,600 pixels. Higher resolution permits additional spatial detail only when the source camera, focus, lighting and encoding preserve that detail. Enlarging a blurred recording does not recover missing detail.

Aspect ratio is the width-to-height relationship. 16:9 is a common landscape format; 9:16 is a vertical format used on phones; 4:3 is sometimes encountered in historical video. Converting 16:9 to 9:16 usually requires cropping or adding unused areas. Cropping can remove dates, record identifiers, captions, navigation items or important context. Evaluate a proposed crop instead of automatically filling every screen.

In a screen tutorial, zoom or crop carefully: retain the relevant buttons, field labels, cursor and information needed to repeat the task. In an archive-related video, never silently crop a photograph in a way that changes evidential meaning; identify explanatory close-ups as such.

5.7 Shot types, camera movement and composition

A wide shot establishes the setting—for example, the entrance to a library. A medium shot shows the person and a relevant action, such as a librarian at a service desk. A close-up reveals a detail, such as a visible barcode or a button on a demonstration screen, using fictional details where appropriate. A sequence may combine wide → medium → close-up to move from general context to specific information.

Compose each shot deliberately. Keep the important subject well lit and within focus. The rule of thirds is a common guide to placement, but it is not compulsory: centred framing may work better for a direct instructional demonstration. When recording by phone, hold it steadily, keep the intended orientation, avoid excessive digital zoom and monitor the background.

Continuity means that adjacent shots are understandable together. If an object is on the left in the first shot and unexpectedly on the right in the next, viewers may become confused. Video for professional training should clearly label reenactments, simulated cases and scenes whose order has been modified.

5.8 Light, exposure, focus and stability

Light makes an image possible. Exposure controls how bright captured content appears; overly dark footage hides details and overexposed highlights can remove information. If filming a person by a very bright window, either reposition them, adjust camera exposure or use a gentler light source. Diffused daylight and a stable device are often sufficient for simple student work.

Focus determines which plane looks sharp. Ensure that the text, screen or object that matters is clearly visible. Test the result on the display size used by your intended audience, not only on the small camera preview. Avoid reflections that conceal labels on paper or computer screens.

Stabilisation reduces unwanted camera movement. A tripod is useful, but resting a phone on a stable surface also works. For tabletop record-handling demonstrations, avoid risky filming setups that might damage source materials. Check ten seconds of footage before recording the whole session.

5.9 Recording and mixing sound for video

Viewers often tolerate modest picture quality if speech is clear, but struggle with a beautiful video whose narration cannot be understood. In a quiet setting, place the microphone consistently, record a test, listen on headphones and avoid clipping. If your editor shows sound levels, check for sudden peaks and long silent sections. Background music is optional and should never overpower instructions. Use only sound that you created, licensed or are otherwise authorised to use.

Prepare important spoken names and technical terms carefully; automatic recognition systems are more likely to mishear them. Good audio production supports good captions. Do not use heavy noise filtering if it would remove historically meaningful sounds or introduce misleading artefacts in an archival recording.

5.10 Video editing: the timeline and its operations

In non-linear editing software, video and audio are arranged on a timeline. Video tracks may hold recorded footage, screen captures, titles and graphics. Audio tracks may hold speech, natural sound and authorised music. Each clip occupies a time interval. You can usually move or trim a clip without modifying the original source media.

Core operations include trim (shorten a clip's beginning or end), split (divide one clip into two timeline items), cut (move abruptly to another shot), fade (gradual transition in sound or picture), crossfade (overlap audio or pictures), colour correction (adjust exposure and colour consistently) and export/render (produce a standalone delivery file).

A good edit removes avoidable confusion while preserving meaning. A records-management video used as evidence may need stricter controls and source retention than a promotional library introduction. Keep an edit log recording the source file name, important cuts or overlays, editor, date, reason for changes and export settings.

Editing checklist

  1. Copy recordings into an organised project folder and confirm their playback before editing.
  2. Decide the sequence and align it with the objective and storyboard.
  3. Remove only material that can be omitted without distorting the message; use transitions sparingly.
  4. Add clear titles and labels without covering evidence or interface controls.
  5. Mix narration at an understandable level and check speech throughout.
  6. Add accurate captions and descriptions; verify their timing.
  7. Export a viewing version and watch it through on a target device.
  8. Retain the appropriate source, project, caption and metadata files in separate, clearly named folders.

5.11 Animation and motion graphics

Animation creates movement by designing frames or changing visual properties over time. It may be hand drawn, computer-generated or created from separate physical photographs (stop-motion). In 2D motion graphics, arrows, icons, diagrams and labels move to make relationships visible. 3D animation constructs an apparent three-dimensional scene. Not every video needs animation: use it only when it clarifies information.

A keyframe specifies a property at a particular moment—for instance, an icon starting at horizontal position 50 pixels and ending at 450 pixels after two seconds. Software interpolates the positions between keyframes. With linear timing, the icon moves at roughly constant speed; with easing, it starts or ends gradually. The interactive motion demonstration lets you compare discrete samples at different frame rates without requiring advanced software.

An appropriate BRIM application is an animated records life cycle, with labelled stages for creation or receipt, classification, active use, retention review and authorised transfer or disposal. This must remain an illustration: actual retention decisions depend on approved schedules and legal requirements. Another BLISS example is an animated sequence showing how a book request passes from search results to a collection point.

Use motion responsibly. Fast flashing or unnecessary looping can cause discomfort. Allow content to be paused, avoid relying on animation alone to communicate meaning and provide a static description or equivalent text where useful.

5.12 Captions, subtitles and transcripts

Captions are synchronised text representations of speech and important non-speech audio. They can identify a speaker, indicate laughter, a door closing or another sound needed to understand the scene. Translated subtitles present dialogue in a different language. In some regions the words “captions” and “subtitles” are used interchangeably. A transcript is a readable text version of spoken and other relevant sounds; a descriptive transcript also describes important visual information.

Captions serve viewers who are Deaf or hard of hearing, and also people who are studying in a quiet room, learning a new language or using a device without working speakers. Automatically generated captions are a useful draft, not a verified final product. Misrecognised course codes, names, accents, dates and technical words can alter meaning. Review against the actual recording and a known authoritative script where possible.

For accessible prerecorded video with audio, accurate captions are an important requirement under WCAG 2.2 criterion 1.2.2. For visual information that narration does not explain, consider audio description or integrated spoken description. Describe what matters instead of merely saying “as shown on screen”. A descriptive transcript provides another way to access the complete meaning.

For guidance see the W3C resource on captions, transcripts and description of visual information.

5.13 WebVTT caption structure and timing

A common browser-compatible text caption format is Web Video Text Tracks (WebVTT), usually stored as a .vtt file. A simple caption file begins with WEBVTT, followed by timed cues. For example:

WEBVTT

00:00:00.200 --> 00:00:05.800
Find a resource in the library catalogue.

00:00:06.200 --> 00:00:11.800
Enter a title or subject in the search box.

Each cue should have a start time earlier than its end time. Captions should appear in the correct order, avoid unwanted overlaps and remain long enough to read. The examples above are simple cues; full WebVTT allows more options. Good captioning also requires appropriate line lengths, punctuation, speaker information and readable timing. The site's caption checker is an educational validator for common timing errors, not a complete WebVTT conformance checker or substitute for human review.

You can download and edit the example unit5_training_captions.vtt file, and compare it with the original training clip in the player. For practice, deliberately change a time range, run the checker, identify the problem, and restore the correct cue.

5.14 Video formats, codecs and bitrate

A container packages streams and metadata. MP4, MOV, MKV and WebM are examples of containers. A codec determines how streams are encoded and decoded; H.264/AVC and H.265/HEVC are video codecs, while AAC and Opus are audio codecs. An .mp4 extension does not tell you every codec inside the file. Compatibility must be tested with actual devices and browsers.

Video compression reduces data size by exploiting redundancy within and across frames. Lossy compression discards some information, trading exact fidelity for smaller files. Repeated export and recompression can degrade a source; retain a suitable higher-quality version when long-term reuse is required.

Bitrate counts encoded bits per second. A useful approximate storage formula for a constant or average total bitrate is:

Estimated size in MB = total bitrate (Mb/s) × duration (seconds) ÷ 8.

For a 120-second video whose average video bitrate is 3.0 megabits per second and audio bitrate is 0.128 Mb/s, the combined rate is 3.128 Mb/s. Estimated size = 3.128 × 120 ÷ 8 = 46.92 MB (decimal), about 44.75 MiB. Actual size can differ due to variable bitrate, metadata and file packaging.

A higher bitrate is not always required for simple slides or static screen recordings. Complex motion usually needs more data to maintain comparable visible quality. For low-bandwidth audiences, choose a tested delivery format and give a transcript and downloadable resources that avoid unnecessary data consumption.

5.15 Source masters, working projects and access copies

Source media are the original recordings, screen captures, photographs and audio tracks. Working/project files preserve edit decisions, relationships between clips and reusable graphics. An access copy is an export chosen for practical viewing, such as a compressed MP4. These three categories should not be confused.

A convenient, compressed MP4 may be appropriate for student viewing but may not be the only file to retain for preservation. The Library of Congress Recommended Formats Statement for moving images distinguishes higher-quality video source/final production material from viewing proxies and names formats such as FFV1/Matroska and ProRes in particular professional settings. There is no universal single preservation format for every organisation: local capacity, rights, source quality and preservation policy matter.

For any professional project, record the relationship between source, edited output, accessible derivatives and descriptive documents. If the material has evidential significance, take steps to establish provenance, protect the source against accidental alteration, document edits and keep access restrictions with the managed record.

5.16 Metadata, naming and organisation

A good video can be difficult to discover or trust without basic metadata. Include a meaningful title, content summary, creator or responsible unit, date, duration, language, subject keywords, identifier, rights/consent status and intended access group. Technical metadata should record the pixel dimensions, frame rate (or variable-rate nature), container and codecs, approximate bitrate and file size where relevant.

A simple folder arrangement is:

unit5_project/
  01_source/  camera files, original audio, references
  02_working/  script, storyboard, editing project
  03_access/  final_access.mp4, final_captions.vtt
  04_metadata/  description.txt, rights.txt, edit_log.txt

Keep public access items separate from restricted source material. A file name such as library_orientation_access_v1_2026-11-04.mp4 is more informative than finalfinal2.mp4, but file names alone are not a preservation or security programme.

5.17 Ethical and authentic storytelling

A coherent story is not a licence to invent facts. If a historical event is reconstructed, label it as a reconstruction. If animation illustrates a disputed or unknown detail, make that uncertainty visible. Avoid creating a fictional quote and attributing it to a real interviewee, even when the narration sounds plausible. Never publish a real student's identity, a person in a recording, a restricted file or copyrighted third-party content without the relevant authority.

A BLISS service video might legitimately emphasise simplicity, speed and learner confidence. A BRIM archive exhibition may need more explanatory context about provenance, date, custody and documentary evidence. Both benefit from checking captions for names and dates, preserving authorised source material and giving viewers ways to verify claims.

5.18 Guided practical: use the supplied training video

Suggested laboratory time: 80–110 minutes. The downloadable unit5_training_clip.mp4 is an original, computer-generated classroom video with four simple scenes and synthetic narration. The .vtt file holds timed captions. A scene-planning CSV and descriptive transcript are also provided. They contain fictional examples only.

  1. Download and play the example video. Write one sentence identifying the audience and the main task it teaches.
  2. Find the beginning and end of each of its four scenes. Compare these with the supplied storyboard.
  3. In the online Shot Explorer, choose a wide, medium and close-up view. Explain which would best show a building, a staff member's action and a catalogue button.
  4. In the Frame Sampler, compare 12, 24 and 30 fps and calculate the frame counts for ten seconds. Explain why a simple moving dot is not proof of all features of real video encoding.
  5. Use the Bitrate Calculator to estimate sizes for a two-minute video at different bitrates, then compare the values with a hypothetical student mobile-data allowance.
  6. Copy the provided captions into the Caption Check activity. Check correct timing; change a cue so the end precedes the start and identify the error. Restore it.
  7. In the Storyboard Builder, choose a BLISS or BRIM objective and prepare four scenes. Include a duration, a meaningful visual and an audio/text alternative.
  8. In the Release Decision activity, explain why permission and authentic source handling are needed before publication.
  9. Write a simple metadata record and a short quality-assurance checklist.

A student with a suitable phone or laptop editor can complete the extension task by producing their own short, permission-cleared clip. If equipment is limited, students can evaluate and re-caption the supplied synthetic resource, then submit a realistic storyboard and edit plan. Do not use unauthorised recordings.

5.19 Assignment: make a 60–90 second service video

Choose one of the two pathways:

  • BLISS — Library orientation/tutorial: explain a specific task (locate a catalogue item, navigate the digital library or identify an authorised service). Use a clear instructional sequence and test with a peer who has not seen the procedure.
  • BRIM — Archives/records demonstration: demonstrate a fictional records handling workflow or interpret an approved digital exhibit. Preserve context, cite sources, document simulations and keep any genuine restricted material out of the final public video.

Required deliverables: (a) objective and audience; (b) four-scene storyboard and script; (c) 60–90 second access MP4 or approved alternative; (d) checked .vtt captions and descriptive transcript; (e) technical/descriptive metadata; (f) edit and permissions log; and (g) a short reflection on usability, authenticity and mobile access. The sample work can be used where recording is impossible, but clearly mark it as the classroom demonstration.

Illustrative formative assessment (20 marks): purpose/storyboard 4; production and coherent editing 5; captions and accessible description 4; metadata/permissions/source control 4; user test/reflection 3. This practice rubric does not override any official assessment arrangement.

5.20 Review and further study

Before leaving the unit, ask yourself: Can I distinguish 1920 × 1080 from 30 fps? Can I explain why a small compressed MP4 is not necessarily a suitable sole archive copy? Can I calculate approximate frames and file sizes? Can I identify a misleading scene crop? Can I write and check caption timing? Could another student reproduce my storyboard and understand the video without hearing it? Can I prove which source clips and permissions were used?
