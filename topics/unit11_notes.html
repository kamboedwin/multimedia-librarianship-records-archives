Legal, Ethical and Inclusive Multimedia Practice

Rights, privacy, dignity, accessibility and responsible technology choices in libraries and archives.

← Back to Unit 11 on the course site

Start here: a familiar situation

A library has a striking photograph of a student event and wants to post it publicly. Owning a copy of the photograph does not automatically establish copyright permission, and copyright permission does not automatically resolve privacy or consent issues for people pictured. The published page also needs readable text, suitable image alternatives and an accurate account of its source.

Key words in simple language

  • Copyright: legal protection for qualifying original expression.
  • Licence: permission to use a work under stated conditions.
  • Personal data: information concerning an identified or identifiable person.
  • Accessibility: design that lets people with different abilities use information.

Think about it: Why can an image be technically easy to post but inappropriate to publish? The sections below explain how to answer this question.

For other terms, see the course multimedia glossary.

11.1 The professional meaning of responsible multimedia

Multimedia publication is a decision to make information accessible to a chosen audience. That decision affects creators, people represented in the content, data subjects, institutions, and future users. Technical capacity to upload or copy a file should not be confused with legal authority to distribute it. A librarian may own a physical photograph without owning the photographer’s copyright. An archive may hold a valid institutional record while being required to limit who can see its contents. A video can be factually accurate yet inaccessible to a user who cannot hear its narration.

A useful ethical review asks five connected questions. What legitimate information-service purpose is being served? What rights or permissions are needed for the intended reuse? What personal information is involved and on what basis may it be processed or disclosed? Can users with different abilities and devices understand and use the content? Is the content accurate, properly contextualised and represented honestly? Every answer should have evidence, not only an opinion or a design preference.

In a combined BLISS and BRIM class, the same framework works in different settings. A library publishes a digital exhibition to educate the public; a records office provides access to selected institutional documents while preserving provenance and statutory restrictions. Both aim for useful access, but neither can assume that “digitised”, “historic” or “educational” means unrestricted publication.

11.2 Copyright and neighbouring rights

Copyright protects eligible original expression in many works used in multimedia, such as writing, photography, music, software and audiovisual works. A fact about a historical event is not automatically protected in the same way as a creative photograph showing that event. Copyright can arise from creation without registration, subject to the applicable legal system. Physical ownership and copyright ownership must be analysed separately: receiving a printed photo does not automatically transfer the right to publish that photo online.

For Tanzania, a primary teaching reference is the Copyright and Neighbouring Rights Act, Chapter 218, Revised Edition 2023. The Act addresses copyright and neighbouring rights, but the application of any exception or limitation requires attention to the facts and the exact provision. Do not reduce the legal analysis to an invented rule that any classroom or educational purpose permits unlimited reproduction. Different activities—scanning, translating, editing, distributing a recording and public web display—can require separate consideration.

A robust rights register identifies the item, creator or known rights holder, the original source, the evidence of licence or permission, permitted reuse, attribution, limits, expiry or review conditions, and the person who approved the decision. This register should remain connected to the item metadata. If ownership is uncertain, the issue should be recorded as unresolved rather than guessed. Professional advice may be required for real institutional materials.

11.3 Licensing and Creative Commons

Creative Commons offers standardised public licences with conditions. CC BY allows reuse and adaptation, including commercial use, with attribution. CC BY-SA adds a share-alike obligation when sharing adaptations. NC indicates a restriction on commercial uses. ND means that adaptations may not be shared under that licence without additional permission. CC BY-NC, CC BY-NC-SA and CC BY-NC-ND combine the relevant elements. The exact licence version matters, and the human-readable summary does not replace the legal text.

An attribution should identify the creator, title when supplied, source, licence and changes. A model fictional credit is: “Reading room entrance”, A. Example, CC BY 4.0, cropped from original, linked to the source and licence. Merely writing “Image from Google” does not give a reliable creator, source or permission basis. CC0 is not another of the six CC licences: it is a public-domain dedication tool. A public-domain claim should also be checked against its jurisdiction and the specific object.

Licensing does not cancel privacy, personality, confidentiality or data-protection requirements. A photograph licensed by its photographer may still depict an identifiable person whose image should not be published in that context. The safest student exercise uses original fictional media, clearly cleared resources or a purposeful request for permission. A rights decision is complete only when the specific intended acts fit the licence and other restrictions.

11.4 Personal data, purpose and lawful processing

Personal data may consist of names, identification numbers, faces, voices, locations, contact details or contextual clues that identify someone. An oral-history recording may include information about people who never spoke to the researcher. Video metadata can reveal the location or date of capture. A scanned form may disclose a signature, private address or medical information even if the visible page title seems harmless. The risk is the information revealed, not only the file format.

Tanzania’s Personal Data Protection Act, 2022 and the Personal Data Collection and Processing Regulations, 2023 provide the national context for collecting, using and disclosing personal data. A professional workflow identifies the purpose, a lawful basis, data minimisation, retention responsibilities, access permissions, appropriate security and a review process. Consent may be relevant in some cases, but it is not the only possible legal basis and should not be treated as a universal cure for otherwise excessive or unlawful processing.

The permission to retain records and the decision to publish them are not identical. An institution may have a legal responsibility to retain confidential employee documents while making only a public finding aid available. For an interview, documenting who can hear the recording, whether online publishing was explained, which segments are restricted and how future questions are handled gives more reliable protection than assuming any signed form grants unlimited rights.

11.5 Consent, children and potentially vulnerable people

Meaningful informed consent should connect a person’s choice to a clearly described purpose and foreseeable uses. Useful information includes whether the material will appear on an unrestricted website, whether it will be edited or translated, whether third parties may download it, and how the institution handles requests or changes where legally applicable. A recording permission is not automatically equivalent to a permission to share the recording publicly. Keeping an audit trail of the consent discussion and version of a permission form protects the person and the institution.

Images or recordings involving children and vulnerable people call for particularly careful assessment. Relevant parental or guardian permissions, safeguarding policy, legal requirements and the best interests of the child need to be checked. Avoid including names, precise locations or identity documents without necessity. These teaching materials therefore use fictional persons, mock records and simulated rights issues rather than actual sensitive student or personnel data.

Libraries and archives may also steward culturally sensitive material. Community expectations, historical injustice, contextual harm and appropriate consultation can matter even when conventional rights ownership appears clear. Ethical practice may support contextual notices, consultation, restricted reading-room access or delayed release. The correct response is reasoned, documented and proportionate rather than an automatic publish-or-destroy binary.

11.6 Inclusive design and WCAG concepts

An inclusive multimedia service recognises differences in vision, hearing, movement, literacy, language, device quality and network availability. Accessibility should be planned at the beginning of the project: choose readable type, semantic headings, clear navigation, keyboard-operable controls and sufficient contrast. A text-first path can benefit someone using a screen reader and someone paying for mobile data. Accessibility testing should include real tasks such as finding a catalogue item, starting a video and reading its transcript.

WCAG 2.2 describes testable accessibility success criteria. For Level AA, normal-sized text generally requires a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1; large-scale text generally requires at least 3:1. These figures do not replace judgement about poor typography, tiny fonts or visual overload. Important non-text interface components may also require sufficient contrast. Users should not have to infer the meaning of a warning using red-versus-green colour alone.

Keyboard testing is an effective low-cost check. Starting with the mouse unused, a student should be able to reach the main navigation and all interactive activities with Tab and activate relevant controls. The focused item should be visible and the order understandable. A heading or button that visually resembles an action but cannot be reached with the keyboard creates a barrier. Automated tools can detect some errors, but cannot judge every user need.

11.7 Captions, transcripts, audio descriptions and alternatives

Captions communicate dialogue and meaningful non-speech sounds that people need to understand synchronised media. Subtitles often focus on spoken language; therefore, translating dialogue alone may omit speaker identity, laughter, alarms or other important sounds. A transcript provides a textual version of audio content. For a video in which key actions are only shown visually, a descriptive transcript may also need the essential visual information.

Audio description communicates visual information in a form accessible to people who cannot see the video adequately. It might explain that an archivist places a red confidential stamp on a folder while the original audio is silent. Well-planned narration can sometimes include these details naturally, reducing the need for a separate description track. W3C guidance distinguishes the uses of captions, transcripts, audio description and accessible media players.

Example: a library publishes a two-minute orientation video. It includes speech captions, a short transcript, a meaningful description of the floor-map animation, controls that can be operated by keyboard and a static text guide for a low-bandwidth user. The task is not simply to add captions after exporting: each medium should convey the purpose to people who use it.

11.8 Authenticity, representation and responsible AI

Artificial intelligence can help generate first drafts of captions, transcriptions, translation suggestions, metadata or storyboard ideas. It can also hallucinate missing details, misidentify speakers, alter a historical image or invent events in a summary. Human validation is therefore essential whenever the output is presented as documentary fact or professional guidance. A confident AI response is not evidence that the claim is correct.

The origin of a synthetic image, cloned voice, reconstruction or altered historical scene should not be concealed when it affects interpretation. An exhibition can legitimately include an artist’s imagined historical reconstruction, but should label it accordingly and distinguish it from an original document. Maintain source references, editing logs, review dates and approvals; original records and generated interpretations should not be conflated.

Protected documents or identifiable recordings should not be uploaded to external generative AI services without approval and appropriate assessment of data handling. Consider service retention, access, location, institutional contracts and rights before sending content outside institutional systems. For class practicals, fictional or synthetic materials let students explore an AI-assisted workflow without exposing people or confidential collections. UNESCO’s Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence supports privacy, transparency, human oversight and fairness as core principles.

11.9 Ethical case analysis: from uncertainty to a decision

A professional decision begins with the item and purpose. Suppose an archive holds a historical photograph with no identified photographer, a visible child and notes describing a restricted family case. It would be wrong to conclude immediately that the photograph is public domain merely because it is old. It would also be too simplistic to delete the historical record. A responsible decision logs the uncertainty, seeks provenance, checks rights and privacy, considers a descriptive public catalogue entry and refers the case for appropriate review.

A second example is an interview transcript containing a third party’s phone number. The interviewee agreed to archival research, but there is no evidence that the third party agreed to public disclosure. One possible approach is to retain the controlled source, create an authorised derivative that omits unnecessary identifying details, review whether the recording itself still reveals the information, and document the action. The precise basis for access must be established by the institution, not assumed by the student.

Every recommendation should be proportionate. Sometimes the answer is publish with credit and captions; sometimes the answer is restrict pending permissions, add contextual information, create a safer substitute or seek professional guidance. A short decision register that records facts, uncertainties, the chosen action and responsible staff is more valuable than a generic statement that ethics is important.

11.10 Practical documentation and publication workflow

Before publication, build a register containing each item’s identifier, source, creator, copyright or licence basis, evidence, personal-data classification, consent status if relevant, proposed audiences, accessibility alternatives, permitted actions and approval. A rights register should not publish sensitive consent forms or personal contact details; those belong in protected institutional records. Record the public credit separately from internal rights evidence.

Create access derivatives with careful checks. A visually covered name might still be present in a PDF text layer or in searchable OCR output; metadata or a thumbnail cache may disclose information thought to be removed. Redaction must therefore include verification of the exported artefact and associated delivery versions. Document the source-to-derivative relationship and retain the protected source as policy requires.

Post-publication responsibility also matters. Provide an appropriate way to raise an access concern, rights claim or factual correction. When a complaint is received, record it, protect relevant evidence, route it to responsible staff, assess the content and make justified corrections without silently falsifying original records. Ethical stewardship continues for the lifetime of the service.

11.11 BLISS and BRIM application cases

BLISS case: a library plans a public online exhibition featuring eight fictional historical photographs and a short narrated tour. A suitable submission includes a media register, a rationale for reuse, creator credits and links, image descriptions, an audio transcript, keyboard navigation checks and a small publication decision table. The students should demonstrate that an engaging resource can be both legally reviewed and inclusive.

BRIM case: a records and archives service holds five fictional oral-history recordings and related committee minutes. Some contain protected personal details. The students should distinguish secure retention of originals from access to selected derivatives, document provenance and restrictions, decide which descriptions can be public, show a redaction verification step and explain how users would request controlled access.

Both projects are assessed on evidence. A visually impressive exhibition with unverified rights or no accessibility testing is incomplete. A securely stored collection with no intelligible information access is also incomplete. The aim is integrated professional judgement—purposeful media, accurate description, lawful handling, quality control and appropriately accessible delivery.

11.12 Review: applying ethical decisions to multimedia projects

The central misunderstandings of this unit are worth revisiting. Content appearing in a search engine does not automatically enter the public domain. Crediting a photographer does not always grant permission. A consent form for recording is not necessarily a licence for unlimited reuse. A technically sound video may still be inaccessible. A plausible AI-generated summary is not the same thing as historical evidence.

A useful final checklist is: identify the material and purpose; confirm who may grant rights; verify licences and limitations; consider privacy and consent; check source context; create accessible formats; test the user journey; approve suitable access; retain records of decisions; and plan for corrections. The checklist is not a legal certificate and should be applied alongside current institutional policies and professional advice.

Unit 12 will combine the technical content of earlier units with professional publication, evaluation and a project presentation. The decisions created here—rights evidence, provenance, access, inclusive design and ethical disclosure—should accompany the final project rather than appear as a detached paragraph at the end.
