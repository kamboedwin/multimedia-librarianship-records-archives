LIU08106 / RIU08106 · Unit 05

Review Questions and Applied Exercises

Video fundamentals, storyboards, editing, captions, accessibility and professional stewardship.

← Back to Unit 5 on the course site

Part A — Video principles and planning

  1. Explain the main differences between video, a still image, animation and a slide sequence.
  2. Give two cases in which video is more useful than a written guide, and two cases in which text might be preferable.
  3. Outline the stages of pre-production, production and post-production with two examples of work in each.
  4. Write a measurable learning outcome for a 90-second BLISS e-book search tutorial.
  5. Distinguish a script, a storyboard and a shot list; describe how each contributes to planning.
  6. Prepare a four-scene storyboard for a fictional BRIM records handover demonstration.
  7. Compare the communication purposes of wide, medium and close-up shots.

Part B — Frames, pixels and encoding

  1. A clip runs for 75 seconds at a constant 24 fps. Approximately how many frames does it contain?
  2. A 90-second 30 fps clip is changed to 15 fps while retaining duration. Compare the approximate frame counts.
  3. Explain why a higher frame rate does not automatically mean more readable catalogue text.
  4. Calculate the number of pixels in a 1920 × 1080 frame and compare it with 1280 × 720.
  5. Explain the difference between 16:9 and 9:16 and identify a cropping risk for a historic document.
  6. Distinguish an MP4 container from a H.264 video codec and AAC audio codec.
  7. Estimate the size in decimal MB of a 120 s clip with 3 Mb/s video and 128 kb/s audio. Show working.
  8. Explain why the estimated file size may differ from the actual encoded file size.
  9. What extra checks are appropriate when exporting video for students with low-bandwidth connections?

Part C — Recording, animation and editing

  1. Design a checklist for capturing a readable screen tutorial in a room with window glare.
  2. Explain trimming, splitting, cutting, crossfades, title overlays and export in a video editor.
  3. Describe how an editor could accidentally change the evidential meaning of a source clip.
  4. Distinguish a frame from a keyframe, using an animated diagram example.
  5. Explain linear motion and ease-in/ease-out using a two-second moving icon.
  6. Propose an accessible animated explanation of the records life cycle, with an explicit note on its limitations.

Part D — Accessibility, authenticity and professional cases

  1. Distinguish captions, translated subtitles, a transcript and a descriptive transcript.
  2. Explain why automatic captions require human review, using an example involving names, dates or terminology.
  3. Write two valid WebVTT timed cues for the first 12 seconds of a fictional library tutorial.
  4. Explain how audio description can add information that a caption file alone does not provide.
  5. Contrast source video, editor project files and a small access MP4 with respect to reuse and preservation.
  6. Propose at least eight metadata fields to describe an institutional training video.
  7. An archive has consent to record a participant but not permission to publish online. Explain the correct response.
  8. Design a five-step quality check covering captions, device playback, audio clarity, factual accuracy and accessibility.
  9. Compare how a BLISS library guide and a BRIM archive exhibition differ in provenance and rights requirements.
  10. Assess a 60-second synthetic training clip that fails to label a reconstruction and hides a date in a crop. Propose repairs.
Study guidance: Explain concepts with examples, show calculations and units, and justify choices for the target users. For archival use, address provenance, rights and clear labelling of demonstrations. These questions support practice and are not an official examination paper.
