Introduction to Multimedia and Professional Applications

Understanding how text, images, sound, video and interaction work together in information services.

Start here: a familiar situation

A student opens a digital exhibition about the history of a town. The page contains a short written explanation, an old photograph, an audio recollection and a link to a map. Each part provides information that the others cannot fully communicate. Together they form one multimedia experience. A library might use that experience for public learning; an archive might use it to explain the context of historical records.

Key words in simple language

  • Medium: a way of representing information, such as text, sound or a picture.
  • Multimedia: a purposeful combination of at least two media forms.
  • Interaction: an action by the user, such as choosing a link or playing audio.
  • Access: the ability to find and use information in an appropriate form.

Think about it: Why might a photograph and a spoken memory communicate more than a paragraph alone? The sections below explain how to answer this question.

For other terms, see the course multimedia glossary.

What you should be able to do after this unit.
  1. Define multimedia and explain its major elements and characteristics.
  2. Distinguish multimedia, hypermedia, linear and non-linear presentations.
  3. Differentiate analogue and digital media with everyday examples.
  4. Identify how multimedia is applied in libraries, records offices and archives.
  5. Evaluate the advantages, constraints and design considerations of a multimedia service.
  6. Propose a small multimedia service suited to a particular group of users.

1.1 Why study multimedia in information services?

Information is not always best communicated through paragraphs of text. A library may use photographs to show where services are located, a short video to demonstrate an online catalogue, audio to make an exhibition accessible to more visitors, and interactive links to help users choose the information they need. An archives office might preserve a photograph, a recorded interview and a document that together explain a historical event more fully than any single item could.

Multimedia is the purposeful combination of two or more media forms—such as text, still graphics or photographs, audio, video and animation—to communicate information or support a user task. Digital multimedia may also allow the user to navigate, select, search, respond or interact. The word purposeful matters: placing unrelated images beside text is not necessarily good multimedia design. The media should complement one another to help an identifiable audience.

TextImagesAudioVideoAnimationInteraction

A familiar example

Consider an online exhibition about the history of a university. A page describes the institution using text; scanned photographs illustrate how buildings changed; a recorded alumnus tells a first-hand story; a map helps visitors locate places; and navigation links let users choose the decade they want to explore. The result is a multimedia information service because the media work together around a common subject and purpose.

Multimedia is important to information professionals because they do more than store information. They select, organise, interpret, protect and provide access to it. The best media choice depends on user needs, the nature of the evidence, the available resources and any legal or ethical restrictions.

1.2 Core elements of multimedia

Each element has particular strengths and limitations. A thoughtful design uses the form that best supports the information being communicated rather than simply adding more media.

Multimedia does not mean “video only”

A narrated slide presentation may combine text, images and audio and therefore be multimedia. A digital library catalogue enhanced with cover images, descriptions and interactive filters can also be multimedia. Conversely, a plain text notice is generally not multimedia even when displayed on a computer screen. The number and role of media forms—not the prestige of the software—determine the description.

1.3 Characteristics of a useful multimedia system

A multimedia system comprises content, equipment, software, delivery mechanisms and people working together to present or manage multiple media types. For a web-based service, equipment might include a camera or microphone for capture, a computer and editing software for preparation, a server for delivery and a user's phone for access.

CharacteristicWhat it meansExample
IntegrationDifferent forms of media belong to a coherent information experience.Photograph, caption and oral testimony all refer to the same archival item.
Digital representationMost modern production and delivery workflows encode media as computer-readable data.A scanned page is saved as an image file.
User-centred communicationThe product helps a defined audience accomplish a particular task.First-year students quickly locate library services.
Potential interactivityDigital products can support choices, searches, controls and feedback.A visitor selects which collection to explore next.
Storage and delivery demandsDifferent files require different amounts of storage, processing and bandwidth.A video may consume much more data than a short text page.
Consistency and reliabilityMedia, labels and navigation must work together without contradictions.The spoken instructions match the visible steps and captions.

Not every multimedia product is interactive: a recorded documentary is multimedia but typically follows a fixed sequence. Interactivity is a powerful possibility, not a strict requirement for all multimedia.

1.4 Multimedia, hypertext and hypermedia

These three terms are related, but they describe different ideas.

ConceptMeaningIllustration
MultimediaCombines media forms to communicate content.A narrated slideshow with photographs and captions.
HypertextText connected through selectable links that lead to other information.Clicking a linked term in an online guide to open its definition.
HypermediaLinked information in which users can navigate across text and other media types.Clicking an exhibit image to open audio testimony, a related document or a video.

Think of multimedia as describing what kinds of media are present and hypermedia as describing a linked structure through which users can navigate media. Many modern websites are both. However, a video shown from beginning to end is multimedia without being hypermedia, and a text-only set of linked articles may be hypertext without requiring images or sound.

Applied comparison

Online library exhibition

Multimedia: text labels, item photographs and recorded commentary communicate the history of a collection. Hypermedia: visitors select an object, open a related interview, return to a thematic collection and follow links to other items. The same service may display both properties.

1.5 Linear, non-linear and hybrid presentations

In a linear presentation, content follows a predetermined sequence. Users may pause or replay it, but the information is designed to unfold mainly in a fixed order. A documentary film or a traditional narrated slideshow is commonly linear.

In a non-linear presentation, the audience can choose different paths through the content. A digital museum exhibition, catalogue or linked subject guide allows users to move according to their interests. A hybrid presentation combines a guided sequence with menus or alternative branches.

Start→Part 1→Part 2→End
Home↗Collections↔Interviews↙Timeline

Which is better? Neither is universally superior. A short demonstration may be easier to understand in a fixed order. A large information resource usually benefits from navigation and search. For people with limited connectivity, a simple page with clear headings may outperform a complicated interactive display.

1.6 Analogue and digital media

Analogue media represent information through continuously varying physical signals or properties. Examples include sound recorded as variations on magnetic tape, a photographic film negative and sound waves transmitted to a traditional microphone. Digital media represent sampled, encoded or otherwise structured information through discrete values that computers can store and manipulate—often as binary data.

AspectAnalogue exampleDigital example
RepresentationPhysical, continuously varying signal or material.Discrete encoded data.
AudioSpeech on a cassette tape.Voice recording as WAV or MP3.

How does digitisation work?

Digitisation converts analogue material into a digital representation using capture equipment such as a scanner, camera or audio interface. A scanner measures light reflected from a page and encodes it into a grid of pixels. For audio, an analogue-to-digital converter measures the signal at regular intervals; the resulting samples are stored numerically. The exact capture settings and file format influence quality and future reuse, which will be explored in later units.

Analogue original→Capture device→Digital file→Metadata + quality check→Preserved master / access copy

Important distinction: scanning a document changes its representation, not necessarily its meaning or legal status. A scanned document does not automatically become an authentic original, and scanning alone does not produce searchable text. Optical character recognition (OCR) may convert a picture of printed characters into machine-readable text, but OCR results need checking.

1.7 Technologies behind multimedia production and access

Multimedia has a production side and an access side. On the production side, devices capture material, editing software refines it, and storage systems organise it. On the access side, a site, catalogue, repository, application or display delivers the resource to users.

Capture equipment

Camera, scanner, microphone, video recorder, smartphone and analogue-conversion equipment.

Editing and authoring

Image editors, audio editors, video editors, presentation software and website authoring tools.

Organisation and storage

Folders, databases, repositories, asset management systems, metadata and storage devices.

Delivery and access

Web browsers, mobile devices, kiosks, projectors, catalogue interfaces and assistive technologies.

These components need not be expensive. A smartphone, an accessible HTML page and a simple file organisation scheme can support a useful small project. The required tools should follow the learning objective, content sensitivity and available resources. High-cost production is not automatically higher-quality communication.

1.8 Professional applications: BLISS and BRIM

Applications in libraries and information studies (BLISS)

Libraries use multimedia to improve discovery, teaching and engagement. A digital exhibition brings together images, contextual descriptions and audio. An orientation guide can combine a map, short instructional video and searchable FAQs. Information-literacy instruction can use demonstrations to show how search terms, databases and citation tools work. A children's reading promotion may use illustrations and narration, but its design must remain appropriate for the intended audience.

Applications in records and archives management (BRIM)

Records and archives professionals may digitise selected items to support access without frequent physical handling. Oral-history recordings preserve testimonies, while archival descriptions supply provenance and context. A historical collection may link scanned records, photographs, interviews and timelines. A records management training resource may use diagrams and short clips to explain retention, access permissions or records transfer procedures.

Professional taskBLISS applicationBRIM application
Information discoveryMultimedia subject guides and catalogue enrichment.Browsable archival finding aids and contextual exhibits.
Public educationLibrary orientation, information literacy and reading promotion.Records-handling awareness and public archival education.
Community memoryLocal-history digital collections.Recorded oral histories linked to documented sources.
Access to originalsDigital access to fragile library materials where lawful.Access copies of archival records subject to permissions and restrictions.
Professional accountabilityAccurate descriptions, lawful reuse and quality assurance.Provenance, integrity, authenticity and controlled access.
Common responsibility, different emphasis. Both fields require trustworthy information and responsible access. BLISS applications may emphasise user learning and discovery, while BRIM applications may give greater emphasis to evidential context, provenance, retention, restricted access and authenticity. These are tendencies, not exclusive responsibilities.

1.9 Advantages of multimedia in information services

  1. Improved explanation. A video demonstration can make a procedure more understandable than instructions alone; a chart can show relationships quickly.
  2. Stronger engagement. Appropriately chosen photographs and testimony can make a historical collection more meaningful.
  3. Multiple ways to access information. Text, captions, transcripts and accessible media controls help serve diverse needs.
  4. Distance access. Users may consult collections and instructions away from the physical institution.
  5. Reduced handling of fragile originals. Good digital access copies can reduce unnecessary handling, though some tasks still require the original.
  6. Improved interpretation and discovery. Links and metadata connect related resources, people, places and themes.

These are potential benefits, not guarantees. Poorly designed multimedia can distract, exclude or mislead. Effectiveness should be judged by how well users achieve their task.

Accessibility is more than adding subtitles

An inclusive service may require keyboard-operable controls, sufficient text contrast, headings, descriptive link labels, an appropriate alternative for meaningful images, captions for synchronised video and transcripts for audio content. The exact alternative depends on the media and its purpose. These principles are treated more fully in Unit 7 and Unit 11.

Ethics and trustworthy representation

Images and video can be cropped or edited to improve clarity, but editing must not misrepresent what a record shows. In archives, a master copy should be retained, and changes to access copies should be documented. When handling interviews, personal information or restricted records, publication may require additional permissions and access controls.

1.11 A simple multimedia project workflow

Even a small multimedia resource benefits from deliberate planning. The following workflow applies to a library orientation resource or an archival exhibition.

  1. Identify the need and audience. What problem does the user experience, and what outcome should change?
  2. Select appropriate content. Gather accurate sources and confirm that material can be used.
  3. Choose media deliberately. Decide whether text, images, audio, video or interaction are needed and explain why.
  4. Plan the sequence or navigation. Draw a simple storyboard, page map or viewing sequence.
  5. Prepare and integrate assets. Capture or collect media, document sources and build a consistent experience.
  6. Check quality and accessibility. Verify facts, links, caption accuracy, readability and use on relevant devices.
  7. Publish or deliver responsibly. Provide suitable access while respecting copyright, privacy and other restrictions.
  8. Evaluate and maintain. Observe whether users accomplish the task; update material and preserve the underlying assets.
Worked example · BLISS

Helping new students locate library services

Need: new students cannot easily find the circulation desk and electronic resources support. Audience: first-year students with varied phone and connectivity access. Media: short headings and a map for fast scanning, two annotated photographs for recognition, and an optional small demonstration video. Navigation: choose “Borrowing”, “Catalogue” or “Help”. Quality check: ask three students to find the borrowing desk and note where they hesitate. Responsible choice: make written instructions available even if the video cannot load.

Worked example · BRIM

Presenting a local-history collection

Need: visitors need context for digitised municipal photographs and records. Audience: researchers and members of the public. Media: item images, short captions, a chronological timeline and a permitted oral-history excerpt with transcript. Navigation: select a decade or a theme. Quality check: confirm dates and descriptions against source information. Responsible choice: retain unmodified masters, identify provenance and publish only material cleared for access.

1.12 Deciding whether a multimedia design is effective

A multimedia product is successful when it helps its intended users. For a small project, use five questions:

  1. Purpose: Can a user tell what the resource is for?
  2. Clarity: Does each media element communicate useful information rather than decoration?
  3. Navigation: Can users find what they need and recover if they choose a wrong path?
  4. Inclusion: Can users with different devices, abilities and connectivity conditions access the essential message?
  5. Trust and stewardship: Are sources reliable, rights respected, descriptive context adequate and important files protected?

The answer may require a simple revision. For example, replacing an autoplay video with a clear summary and a user-initiated video can make the resource faster, less distracting and easier to control.

Check your understanding

  1. A museum video has sound, images and captions but plays from beginning to end. Is it multimedia? Is it hypermedia? Explain.
  2. A web catalogue contains links but only text descriptions. Explain the difference between hypertext and multimedia in this case.
  3. Why is a scanned record not automatically searchable or legally equivalent to an original?
  4. For a low-bandwidth student, what could you provide alongside a library tutorial video?
  5. Identify one example where your programme would emphasise discovery and another where it would emphasise evidential context.
