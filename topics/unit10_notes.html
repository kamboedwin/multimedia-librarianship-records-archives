Digital Preservation, Security and Continuity

Keep multimedia resources intact, accessible and accountable through time, change and disruption.

Start here: a familiar situation

A library holds digitised photographs on one computer. When the disk fails, the images disappear unless other dependable copies exist. Even if copies exist, the library must know which copy is correct, who may access it, whether it can be opened and whether restoration actually works. Long-term preservation is a continuing set of checks and decisions, not one backup operation.

Key words in simple language

  • Digital preservation: ongoing actions that keep digital material usable and trustworthy.
  • Backup: a separate copy intended to support recovery.
  • Fixity: evidence that recorded bits have or have not changed.
  • Restore test: a practical check that a saved copy can actually be recovered and opened.

Think about it: What does a successful restore test prove that merely making a backup does not? The sections below explain how to answer this question.

For other terms, see the course multimedia glossary.

Learning outcomes. By the end of this unit you should explain core preservation goals; assess loss and security risks; design independently protected copies; interpret fixity results; determine RPO and RTO; evaluate access and migration decisions; and document a test restoration for a BLISS or BRIM collection.

10.1 What digital preservation means

Digital preservation is the planned, active management of digital materials so that they remain understandable, authentic enough for their intended purpose, accessible to authorised users and usable despite technical and organisational change. It is not equivalent to copying files to a hard disk. A stored file can remain perfectly intact while the software needed to read it disappears or the institution loses the description that explains what it is.

A digital library may need to keep a high-quality scan, catalogue record, descriptive caption, rights information and a smaller reading copy. An institutional archive also needs reliable provenance, record order, preservation events, retention decisions and restrictions. Preserving a file without these relationships can make a collection unusable or untrustworthy.

Preservation activities involve appraisal, capture, ingest, checks, storage, metadata, security, monitoring, migration and the controlled provision of access. The tasks continue throughout a collection's life: they cannot be completed by one-time purchase.

10.2 Preservation goals and competing requirements

Integrity means the digital content has not changed unexpectedly. Fixity checks support integrity by comparing a newly computed digest with a recorded digest. Authenticity is the evidential confidence that an object is what it claims to be, based on provenance, documentation and institutional procedures; it is not established solely by a checksum. Availability means authorised people can obtain a needed resource, while confidentiality limits disclosure to appropriate people.

These goals can compete. A public exhibition must be easy to view, but an archived personnel file should not be exposed to visitors. Encryption can protect confidentiality, but a lost decryption key can make the preserved material inaccessible. Professional planning therefore assigns policies and roles, rather than treating all digital content alike.

Think of a digitised historical photograph and a sensitive staff-record scan. Both need long-term preservation and metadata; only the photograph may be approved for public display. The records scan may require access logging, retention controls, restricted account privileges and a reviewed redacted derivative.

10.3 Risks to long-term digital collections

Physical risks include device failure, electrical problems, water, fire, theft and environmental deterioration. Logical risks include bit corruption, incomplete transfers, incorrect deletion, accidental overwriting, ransomware and damaged databases. Technological risks include unsupported codecs, obsolete file formats, broken hyperlinks, expired licences and interactive software that no longer runs.

Organisational risks are equally serious: absent inventory, no budget for recovery, undocumented encryption keys, changes of staff and unclear authority to publish or delete materials. A useful risk register records the asset, event, likelihood, impact, existing controls, owner, treatment and review date. Classroom risk scores are illustrative and should never be presented as verified risk measurements.

For example, a small reading room might prioritise creating a second controlled copy and an item inventory before buying a sophisticated migration system. An electronic records repository with confidential originals may first strengthen privilege management and auditability. The sequence depends on the collection and the consequences of loss.

10.4 A preservation workflow from acquisition to access

A typical sequence is: decide what merits preservation; accept the object; inventory files; validate completeness and formats; establish checksums; record provenance; create geographically and logically independent copies; run scheduled monitoring; plan preservation interventions; provide permitted access. Each stage should produce a record of the action, the responsible person and any exceptions.

At ingest, verify the expected number of files and paths, identify damaged or unsupported material, confirm that filenames and metadata relate to the same object, and create the first trusted fixity baseline. A baseline created after an undetected error will reproduce that error reliably. A preservation service should therefore verify the transfer and provenance, not simply compute hashes.

Preservation masters and access derivatives serve different needs. A master is retained as source evidence under controlled processes. Access derivatives may be smaller, subtitled, compressed, redacted or watermarked. The master must not be silently overwritten when an access copy is edited.

10.5 Fixity checking and SHA-256

A checksum is a value calculated from file bytes. SHA-256 produces a 256-bit digest normally represented by 64 hexadecimal characters. The same input bytes produce the same digest; changing bytes will almost certainly change that value. In a preservation workflow, a trusted expected digest is stored in a manifest and compared with a digest calculated at a later date.

Worked example: a fictional receipt called ARC001.tif has an expected checksum recorded at ingest. At the monthly check, the observed checksum matches; record PASS with check date, tool and operator. Another file ARC002.tif has a different observed checksum; record REVIEW and investigate unexpected modification, corruption, wrong paths or a mistaken baseline. The checksum alone cannot say which cause occurred.

A hash also cannot prove that a file was genuine at the time of ingest or that an authorised person created it. Protect the manifest itself, validate it in the context of provenance and maintain an append-only or otherwise auditable event history. When a mismatch occurs, avoid overwriting the only evidence; locate a verified independent copy and follow the documented incident procedure.

For a local verification on Windows, a student can use certutil -hashfile filename SHA256 in a command prompt; on many Linux and macOS systems, sha256sum filename or shasum -a 256 filename is available. Record the exact command and filename; never paste confidential original data into an unknown website to compute a hash.

10.6 Backups, replication and the 3-2-1 heuristic

A backup is a recoverable point-in-time copy intended to help after deletion, corruption or disaster. Replication creates another current copy, often quickly; it can rapidly propagate erroneous changes or malicious deletion. Archival storage supports long-term retention but may have slow retrieval and different service expectations. The terms are not interchangeable.

The familiar 3-2-1 heuristic suggests at least three copies, on two different kinds of storage or independent systems, with one copy stored off-site. It is a useful starting point, not a universal rule. Independence matters: three copies in one room or under one compromised administrator account may all fail together. Organisations can add offline or immutable copies, distinct credentials, encryption, documented retention and regular restoration tests as appropriate.

Example: primary repository storage at the institution; a controlled backup on a separate local system; and an encrypted off-site copy with access controlled by a different recovery process. The plan names who is authorised to restore, how keys are protected, which backups are retained and how often a sample is restored. A successful scheduled backup notification is insufficient evidence of recoverability.

10.7 Recovery point objective and recovery time objective

RPO (recovery point objective) expresses the maximum acceptable amount of data change that could be lost following an incident, measured in time. RTO (recovery time objective) expresses the intended period for restoring a system or service after disruption. They answer different questions: “How much recent work may be lost?” and “How long can the service remain unavailable?”

Suppose a catalogue is backed up at 08:00, 12:00 and 16:00. If it fails just before 16:00 and the 12:00 backup is the latest verified copy, almost four hours of updates might be lost. This design does not meet a one-hour RPO. If the full tested restore takes seven hours, it cannot currently meet a two-hour RTO.

An RTO must include restoring software, permissions, indexes, configuration and usable service, not only copying files. A successful restore should confirm that users can open and retrieve approved items; a records archive must also validate original order, access rules and preserved audit information.

Worked exercise. Backup interval = 6 hours. Worst-case recent data loss may approach 6 hours, so a 2-hour RPO cannot be met by this schedule alone. If a full restoration test takes 3 hours 20 minutes, it does not yet meet a 2-hour RTO. These are planning examples, not measured performance for an institution.

10.8 Security, least privilege and protected access

Public users typically view approved derivatives; cataloguers edit descriptions; records officers approve restricted access; preservation operators run integrity and migration processes; system administrators manage infrastructure. Each role should have only the privileges essential to its responsibility. High-risk operations, including deleting masters, should use recorded approval and separation of duties where feasible.

Access control requires server-side authorisation, correctly configured storage, authentication and audit records. A hidden page, unpublished URL or client-side JavaScript check is not sufficient protection. In this teaching site, role decisions are only a simulation; no protected records are stored or released by the exercise.

Other important controls include software updates, multifactor authentication, malware protection, encrypted transfers, protected key management, personnel training and monitoring for unusual changes. An institution must apply its own approved policies and any applicable Tanzanian recordkeeping and privacy requirements.

10.9 NIST cybersecurity functions and response planning

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 presents six functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond and Recover. Govern establishes risk policy and accountability; Identify lists assets and exposure; Protect limits harm; Detect finds unusual events; Respond investigates and contains incidents; Recover restores services. These are concurrent outcomes, not steps that must always happen in the listed order.

A ransomware incident might be detected by unusual deletion rates. The response leader can isolate affected systems, preserve evidence and assess scope. Recovery should use a validated copy known to be safe, followed by functionality, data-integrity and access-control tests. Communication, incident reports and updates to future controls also belong in the institutional process.

The correct decision for an individual student is usually to report concerns and follow an authorised procedure, not to independently tamper with a production system or destroy suspected files. The student exercises use invented cases so no personal or sensitive data is exposed.

10.10 Maturity frameworks, formats and sustainability

The National Digital Stewardship Alliance (NDSA) Levels of Digital Preservation offer a structured way to assess and improve preservation capabilities. Version 2.1 released in March 2026 adds environmental sustainability guidance and related revisions. The framework encourages appropriate, evidence-based improvement; the highest level is not an obligatory universal target for every collection.

File formats need regular assessment. A rare codec, complex interactive exhibition or unsupported document application may threaten use even when file integrity is perfect. Format migration transforms content into a better-supported representation. Emulation may be preferable when interactive behaviour or the original computing environment is essential.

Migration necessarily changes bytes, so the new-format file should not be expected to match the original file's checksum. Instead preserve the original when appropriate, record the migration tool and event, generate a new checksum and check significant properties such as legibility, page count, colour, sound sync, metadata and functionality.

Sustainability is a preservation consideration: responsible retention, storage tiering, hardware reuse and measured monitoring can reduce costs and environmental impact. However, cost-saving measures must not undermine the institution's legal duties, essential content, recovery capacity or justified integrity checks.

10.11 Preservation events and documentation

A useful preservation event includes an identifier, date and time, source file identifier, operation type (ingest, check, migration, restore), operator or automated agent, software/version, outcome and notes. It may also link to a ticket, approval or report. These records explain why bytes and formats changed and who was responsible.

A recovery test record should identify the backup selected, recovery environment, checks run, actual time taken, permissions tested, deficiencies discovered and corrective actions. If a fixity check fails, both the failure and response should remain in the audit trail, even after a good copy is restored.

Think of a fictional archival audiovisual package. If the WAV master is migrated to another supported format and a smaller MP3 created for public access, a future custodian must be able to distinguish original, new preservation object and access derivative. Version and provenance links matter as much as filenames.

10.12 BLISS and BRIM preservation scenarios

BLISS digital exhibition: a library holds a group of scanned historic photographs. The preservation plan retains source TIFF masters, descriptive metadata, rights decisions and checksums. Approved access copies are JPEG or web images. The catalogue should remain searchable during routine maintenance, and backup procedures should support restoration of the files and metadata database as a whole.

BRIM electronic records series: a records unit holds digitised meeting documents with administrative restrictions. A recordkeeping system must protect provenance, order, authenticity evidence, rights, retention requirements and event logs. A restore that recovers every PDF but loses the series structure and access permissions is incomplete. Public exhibition and authorised records access therefore require different service designs.

In both cases, staff should perform documented risk reviews, segregate roles, test recovery and follow the relevant institution's legal, privacy and archival rules. The examples are fictional practice cases, not actual institutional records.

10.13 Guided practical and reflection

The supplied package contains four invented files, their expected SHA-256 digest values and a simulated check result. Inspect the manifest and identify which entry warrants investigation. Then design a three-copy plan with independent locations or administrative boundaries and specify which copy would be used in a test restoration.

Set an RPO and an RTO for a proposed library catalogue or records-retrieval service. Explain whether the suggested backup and tested restore schedule could meet those objectives. Assign least-privilege roles, create a small risk register and plan a test restoration with clear pass/fail evidence.

Final reflection: which significant properties must survive? A photo may need faithful colour and the right caption. A recorded interview may need intelligible audio, transcript, permissions and context. A record may need source order, authority and an unbroken change history. Professional preservation is the deliberate management of these requirements over time.

10.14 Backup retention, preservation retention and responsible deletion

Backup retention and archival retention are different decisions. A backup policy may keep hourly versions for a short period and monthly versions for longer, depending on recovery needs and resources. A retention or disposal schedule determines how long a record is legally or administratively required. Keeping an old backup does not authorise keeping restricted information beyond an applicable retention requirement; equally, deleting a production item does not guarantee that every backup copy has been dealt with under the approved policy.

Responsible information governance therefore describes how the preservation service handles appraisal, disposal approvals, retention exceptions, backup expiry and documentation. If an old digital resource remains valuable but can no longer be served publicly, authorised staff may retain the master under controlled access until its status is reviewed. If a confidential record must be disposed of, the correct method depends on institutional policy, legal requirements, backups, replicas and relevant audit records; students should never independently erase operational records as an exercise.

10.15 Extended fixity example: investigating an exception

A training repository receives four text files and creates their SHA-256 digests at ingest. A later integrity log reports that three digests agree and one does not. Before drawing conclusions, a preservation officer checks that the script read the intended path, that the baseline is from the correct ingest event and that no authorised transformation was performed. The officer records the time, software, account, expected and observed digests and the investigation ticket number. A mismatch cannot, by itself, distinguish corruption, the wrong file, deliberate tampering or an incorrect baseline.

Suppose the independent off-site copy matches the trusted baseline and passes a readability test. Following the institution's incident process, an authorised operator may recover the affected file from that verified copy, check all related metadata and confirm that the file is accessible again. The incident record and the original discrepant evidence remain documented. By contrast, if no trustworthy backup exists, overwriting the file merely to make a check display PASS could destroy the only evidence of the problem.

In the downloadable practice batch, the brim_minutes_001.txt source is genuinely intact. The deliberately incorrect digest is stored in a simulated later-check CSV. Students should first compute the authentic hash of the unchanged file and compare it with the expected manifest; only then should they review the simulated exception. This distinction prevents a training exercise from misleading students about the reliability of the source file.

10.16 Expanded recovery exercise: what a successful restore demonstrates

Imagine that a BLISS digital exhibition site becomes unavailable on Monday morning. The team must establish the nature of the disruption, the last working catalogue snapshot and which items have changed since that snapshot. Restoring photographs alone may not return the service: the catalogue database, persistent identifiers, rights notices, image links, user permissions and web configuration must also function. A full restoration test checks at least one item from each media type, confirms that the expected image and metadata display, and records the duration of the work.

For a BRIM archive, the same test is more demanding in another way. The original records must be returned to their correct series, in the correct order and with the same authorised restrictions. The test should verify the preservation event history and identify whether any approvals or user-access audit records were lost. A file list that matches the old directory is insufficient evidence if an unapproved public account can now retrieve a previously restricted record.

A short recovery report therefore distinguishes the planned objective from the tested result: the intended RPO/RTO, selected backup, restore time, tests completed, exceptions and future action. A restoration may be technically successful while failing a business target; this is useful evidence for revising procedures, training or infrastructure.
