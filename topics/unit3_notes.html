Digital Images and Image Editing

Capture with care. Edit transparently. Deliver accessible copies while protecting source information.

← Back to Unit 3 on the course site

Start here: a familiar situation

A librarian wants to publish a page from an old newsletter. The page is first captured using a scanner or camera. One carefully documented copy is retained, while a smaller version is prepared for a website. If the page contains writing, optical character recognition may suggest searchable words, but someone must check the output against the image. Image editing should improve usability without silently changing the historical evidence.

Key words in simple language

  • Capture: creating a digital image from a physical or visual source.
  • Master: a carefully retained source-quality digital version.
  • Derivative: another version made for a specific use, such as web viewing.
  • OCR: software that attempts to recognise text within images.

Think about it: Why is it risky to replace the retained master with a heavily edited JPEG? The sections below explain how to answer this question.

For other terms, see the course multimedia glossary.

Guiding principle. The goal is not simply to make an image look attractive. The goal is to create a useful, faithful, responsibly documented digital resource for a defined audience.

Use the synthetic sample record for the guided practical. It is a classroom illustration and not a real historical record.

3.1 Learning outcomes

By the end of this unit, you should be able to explain image capture methods; select suitable image file formats; perform basic non-destructive editing; distinguish an original/master from an access derivative; describe a quality-control procedure; and explain OCR with its limitations.

In your practical work you will create an image derivative from a training source, keep a clear edit log, assess legibility and provide a checked transcription.

3.2 Digital image production: where images come from

A digital image may originate from a camera, phone, flatbed scanner, overhead camera, document camera, software drawing, or export from an existing digital document. Each method captures or creates visual information differently, so the workflow must begin by identifying the source and intended use.

Camera capture records a scene using a sensor and lens. Good lighting, a steady camera, correct focus and distortion control are essential. For bound volumes or fragile records, an overhead camera with proper support may be preferable to pressing the object onto a flatbed scanner.

A scanner measures reflected or transmitted light across the original and produces pixel samples. A flatbed suits many robust flat originals; a sheet-fed scanner is fast for suitable loose sheets but may damage fragile, stapled or historically important material. Never feed vulnerable originals into equipment without an approved handling procedure.

A screenshot captures the current display presentation, which may be lower quality or omit context compared with a source file. A screenshot is not a substitute for a managed preservation capture.

3.3 Capture quality: resolution, lighting, focus and geometry

Useful image quality depends on the source, capture equipment, operator technique, intended output and subsequent checks. More pixels are not always better if the lens is blurred, the page is warped or important edges are cut off.

Capture resolution can be expressed in pixels per inch (PPI) for an object of known physical dimensions. For example, a 6-inch-wide item sampled at 300 PPI produces approximately 1,800 pixels across. Appropriate settings vary with source dimensions, textual detail, contrast, material condition and institutional quality requirements; 300 PPI is not a universal rule.

For quality control, inspect focus and fine detail at 100% display scale, look for clipped highlights and shadows, check page orientation, assess colour neutrality where relevant, confirm the whole source is included, and ensure names, dates and margins have not been lost.

Capture environments need steady illumination and minimal reflection or glare. Curved pages, shadows, skew, perspective distortion and lens aberration can make information difficult to read. Do not silently alter an original to make it look more attractive at the expense of evidence.

3.4 Image file formats and their trade-offs

The filename extension alone does not establish that an image is authentic, unaltered, high-resolution or suitable for long-term stewardship. A format must be chosen in relation to the content, capture system, software support, preservation policy and intended audience.

JPEG (.jpg) commonly uses lossy compression, often producing smaller files for photographs and web access. Repeated re-encoding or very strong compression can introduce artefacts around text and edges. Avoid using heavily compressed JPEG as the only capture of critical fine-text material.

PNG (.png) uses lossless compression and supports transparency. It is often suitable for diagrams, screenshots and some derivative images; it may be larger than a JPEG for photographs.

TIFF (.tif/.tiff) is a versatile container commonly used in heritage imaging workflows. It can contain uncompressed or compressed image data and can support high-bit-depth captures. TIFF is not automatically lossless: settings, compression, colour encoding and workflow choices still matter.

Other formats include SVG for scalable vector diagrams, camera RAW for sensor-origin capture in some workflows, and WebP or AVIF for optimised web delivery where compatibility is appropriate. Formats are tools, not a simple quality ranking.

3.5 Master files, working copies and access derivatives

A capture or preservation master is a carefully controlled file intended to retain the best appropriate representation from the capture workflow. It should be stored securely with identifying information and not be overwritten casually.

A working copy is used during editing. A derivative is created for a particular purpose, such as a smaller JPEG for a catalogue webpage, a cropped thumbnail for navigation, or a marked-up teaching copy. Derivatives must be distinguishable from the source/master.

Example naming: DEMO-003_master.tif, DEMO-003_access_1600.jpg, DEMO-003_thumb.jpg and DEMO-003_editlog.txt. These names are examples only; a real collection should follow its documented naming and versioning policy.

For BRIM, retaining provenance, original order/context and a record of transformations is especially important when an image may be used as evidence. For BLISS, access copies must remain discoverable, legible and connected to the correct bibliographic description. Both programmes require transparent handling.

Source / master

Preserve the most faithful managed copy; protect it against accidental overwrite.

Working copy

Experiment with adjustments separately, preserving the ability to compare with source.

Access derivative

Optimise size and usability for an audience, document changes and link to source.

3.6 Basic image-editing operations

Crop: remove part of the image frame to create a composition or an access view. Cropping can hide signatures, notes and contextual borders, so retain the complete master and clearly identify a crop as a derivative.

Rotate and straighten: correct sideways pages or small skew to help reading. A rotation may require interpolation and can change pixels. Record substantial corrections in the edit log.

Resize: change image pixel dimensions. Downsampling removes pixel samples, often reducing file size for web use. Upsampling creates additional estimated pixels but does not restore information never captured.

Brightness and contrast: adjust how dark and light regions appear. Excessive adjustment can conceal pencil marks, watermarks or very faint text. View the original beside the derivative, and keep enough tonal range for meaningful interpretation.

Colour correction: adjust colour cast using reliable reference conditions where possible. Sharpening and noise reduction can improve appearance but can also exaggerate edges, remove subtle marks or introduce artefacts.

Annotation: labels, circles and arrows may help public learning. Put interpretive markings on a separate derivative or layer; never mistake the annotated image for the source record.

3.7 Image editing workflow with free tools

GIMP and other locally installed image editors provide tools for practising these techniques. This unit does not require paid software or cloud upload. When documents are confidential, work only on approved institutional devices and storage.

1. Copy the synthetic sample image into a new working folder. 2. Open the image and inspect its size and clarity. 3. Save a working copy without replacing the master. 4. Rotate or straighten only if needed. 5. Make one crop suitable for a website thumbnail and explain what it excludes. 6. Create a readable access image with a sensible width. 7. Save/export the derivative in an appropriate format. 8. Compare with the source and record the settings, file names and reasons.

A good edit log states what was changed, who changed it, when and why. An exemplary entry is: “2026-11-03: resized synthetic sample from 1200 × 800 to 750 × 500; JPEG quality setting 78; purpose: reduced-size teaching access copy; master unchanged.” These are classroom examples, not measurements from a real collection.

3.8 Understanding compression and quality

Lossless compression enables exact restoration of the compressed data after decoding; lossy compression removes some information to reduce size. A format such as PNG commonly uses lossless methods, whereas ordinary JPEG is typically lossy.

Visual quality and saved file size depend on source detail, dimensions, settings, bit depth, metadata, compression algorithm and other factors. A comparison between two files should report both their pixel dimensions and actual measured sizes; comparing only extension names is insufficient.

If a photograph is 1200 × 800 pixels at 24 bits per pixel, its approximate uncompressed sample payload is 1200 × 800 × 3 = 2,880,000 bytes (about 2.75 MiB). A compressed JPEG or PNG may be smaller or larger than another compressed format depending on image content.

3.9 Optical character recognition (OCR)

Optical character recognition is technology that attempts to convert writing visible in an image into machine-readable characters. A scan alone contains pixels; it is not automatically searchable text.

An OCR workflow usually includes capture, orientation/preprocessing, recognition, language or layout handling, verification, correction, and export/storage of both the page image and the text or text layer. OCR engines can fail on faded originals, decorative fonts, unusual scripts, page curvature, handwriting, stains or complex tables.

For example, an OCR result might read “L1BRARY REC0RDS 1984” when the original says “LIBRARY RECORDS 1984”. Human review or carefully designed validation should correct obvious errors and flag uncertain readings. Never assume that a search hit or a clean-looking result proves an error-free transcription.

The language of the document matters. For Kiswahili or bilingual materials, test supported recognition models and manually check diacritics, names, dates and local place names. Where archival fidelity matters, retain the original image and distinguish faithful transcription from any editorial correction.

3.10 Accessibility and responsible image use

An informative image on a webpage should have meaningful alternative text that conveys its purpose, not merely repeat “image”. For a complex archival scan, nearby descriptive text or a transcription may be needed in addition to short alternative text.

Do not crop out source information and then present the result as the complete record. Check copyright, access restrictions, privacy, consent and any institutional rules before publishing original images.

Use captions that identify the item and distinguish a photograph from a constructed illustration or edited reproduction. Public access choices should account for readability on mobile screens, available bandwidth and the needs of users with disabilities.

3.11 Worked example: digitise a historical letter

Scenario: an archive holds a two-page historical letter. Staff wish to provide a readable digital copy for a small exhibition. First, check handling restrictions, rights and physical condition. Record the item identifier and page order.

Capture each page fully, with appropriate support, focus, lighting and sufficient resolution for the smallest meaningful characters. Verify the complete edges and marks are visible. Produce managed masters and record technical information, filenames and capture date.

Create smaller JPEG or PNG access derivatives if suitable, clearly labelled and linked to their corresponding masters. If the public view uses a crop, describe the omitted region and retain access to a complete view where rights permit.

Run OCR when appropriate, compare recognised text against the page, correct demonstrable errors and retain the original page images. Supply a useful caption, alternative text or transcription, and record the relationship among files.

3.12 BLISS and BRIM application cases

BLISS case — digitising library orientation materials: photograph a circulation counter sign, crop a derivative to show its instruction clearly, ensure small-screen readability, include alternative text and explain copyright or permission conditions before publishing.

BLISS case — online reading list: create appropriately sized book-cover thumbnails, preserve links to catalogue descriptions and avoid converting title text into unreadably small image labels.

BRIM case — archival records: capture a dated administrative register, preserve its full frame and record provenance. Make a separate reading copy with moderate contrast enhancement, document adjustments, and check that faint marginal notes remain visible.

BRIM case — oral-history photo collection: describe who created each photograph, when it was taken if known, what is uncertain and how derivatives relate to the retained source material.

3.13 Worked quality-control checklist for image capture

Quality control is a deliberate comparison between the captured file, the original item where it is safe to inspect, and the stated capture specification. It should be planned before production, not added only after a user reports a problem. A capture team may check every file for completeness and sample some technical measurements according to institutional policy. The degree of checking depends on the collection, risk and scale of the project.

3.14 Capture settings versus output settings

Capture and output settings solve different problems. At capture time the aim is to collect sufficient faithful information from the source. Output settings can then be optimised for reading, printing or small-screen delivery. For example, if a high-quality source contains a page 2400 pixels wide, the website may provide a derivative 1200 pixels wide for ordinary viewing. Reducing the website image width may save bandwidth; it should not change the managed capture.

The correct capture settings are not determined only by the resolution of a laptop display. For tiny handwriting, line art, large maps and very small photographs, the relationship between item size, subject detail, equipment and target quality changes. The FADGI technical guidelines provide structured measures of performance, rather than one universal “set every scanner to 600 PPI” instruction. Students should learn to justify a chosen specification and to verify real capture results.

3.15 Editing decisions: a transparent comparison

Consider an imaginary handwritten register. The source capture is complete, but the online preview is too wide for a phone. A reasonable access strategy might downsample the complete image for a fast overview, then offer a separate higher-resolution access copy or zoom facility so users can inspect fine text. This preserves context and usability. A poorer strategy would crop away the date, paste a guessed transcription into the image and delete the full source capture.

Now imagine that the writing is faint. Increasing contrast could help a user read some letters, but an aggressive change might also erase pale pencil marks or merge neighbouring strokes. Therefore, inspect the unchanged source beside any enhanced derivative. Describe the image as “contrast-adjusted for legibility” where that distinction matters, especially for records that may later be cited as evidence.

For BLISS, a book-cover thumbnail might legitimately prioritise a clear title and quick downloading, provided the correct item is identified and publication rights are respected. For BRIM, a historical record thumbnail must not imply that a selected fragment is the complete record. The same editing operation can have different communication and evidential consequences.

3.16 Practical file organisation and security

A simple folder layout for classroom practice can be source/, working/, access/ and logs/. Names should include a stable item identifier and an unambiguous role. A directory convention is helpful, but protection also requires appropriate permissions and backups. A copy on the same failing drive is not a complete backup plan.

When a real collection contains personal records, client information, copyright-restricted material or documents under embargo, do not send those files to public websites or unapproved cloud editors. Use synthetic teaching assets or properly authorised sources. Obtain permission where required, restrict working access, and dispose of temporary copies according to relevant local procedures. Metadata can itself contain sensitive information, so review EXIF location tags, names and comments before public delivery.

3.17 Evaluating your own output

Before submitting an edited image, compare the source and derivative at a sensible magnification, check the derivative on a mobile-width screen, confirm that important text remains readable, and verify the filename and export settings. Measure actual file sizes rather than predicting them from extensions alone. Ensure that the edit log makes each meaningful change understandable to someone who was not present when you made it.

Finally, ask a peer to describe what the image communicates without additional explanation. If the peer mistakes a crop for the complete historical document, cannot read the essential label, or assumes an OCR typo is a genuine name, the output needs improvement. This simple user-centred test is as important as technical export success.

3.18 Common misconceptions and unit summary

“A TIFF is always the best format”: false; suitability depends on intended use, encoding and documented policy. “Every image needs 600 PPI”: false; it depends on the source and measurable needs. “OCR is always accurate”: false; review is essential.

“Cropping an archival image is harmless”: sometimes it removes evidence or context. “Higher brightness restores erased text”: an adjustment may alter presentation but does not guarantee information recovery. “A copied image is preserved”: preservation also needs verification, management and redundancy.

A useful final check is to distinguish a captured image, a controlled master, a working copy and an access derivative; justify a file format; perform an edit with a log; and explain the limits of OCR.
