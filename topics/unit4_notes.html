Audio in Multimedia Systems

Sound capture, editing, accessible delivery, transcription and oral-history stewardship.

← Back to Unit 4 on the course site

Start here: a familiar situation

A visitor who cannot come to the library listens to a recorded exhibition guide. They hear the speaker's voice, but they also need a readable transcript and a clear title. A records office may hold an interview for historical evidence and need much stronger documentation of the recording, people involved and access conditions. Digital audio therefore involves capture, sound quality, description, access and preservation.

Key words in simple language

  • Sound wave: a changing vibration that can be recorded.
  • Sample rate: the number of audio measurements taken each second.
  • Bit depth: the number of bits used to represent a sample in uncompressed digital audio.
  • Transcript: a written representation of spoken words and relevant sounds.

Think about it: What information would a transcript provide to someone unable to hear the recording? The sections below explain how to answer this question.

For other terms, see the course multimedia glossary.

Learning pathway: Understand sound → record or import → evaluate → edit on a copy → export → transcribe → describe and protect. All examples are fictional or synthetic unless clearly stated otherwise.

4.1 Learning outcomes

After studying this unit, you should be able to describe sound and digital audio, explain how microphones and recorders capture sound, calculate uncompressed audio storage, choose appropriate audio formats, produce a short recording, perform basic non-destructive editing, create and verify a transcript, and manage audio with suitable documentation and permission controls.

In the practical assessment, you will present an original voice recording or the provided synthetic demonstration, a cleaned access copy, a checked transcript, a small metadata record and an edit log. You will justify your choices for a library or a records-and-archives setting.

4.2 What is audio in a multimedia information service?

Audio is information represented by sound. In multimedia systems, audio may carry speech, pronunciation, music, environmental sound, alerts or evidence of an event. Audio is especially valuable when tone, accent, emotion, pauses or ambience communicate information beyond the printed words.

A library may publish an accessible recorded orientation, an audio guide to a digital exhibition, author interviews or short pronunciation examples. An archive may manage oral-history interviews, meeting recordings, radio broadcasts and field recordings. The technical process can be similar, but professional requirements differ: a user guide is often edited for clarity, whereas an archival interview must retain provenance, context and a defensible record of changes.

Audio rarely works alone in an effective information service. Provide descriptive titles, an understandable playback interface, information about the speaker and recording context where permitted, and a transcript or another appropriate text alternative. In some cases, a short summary and section timestamps improve retrieval.

4.3 Physical sound: vibrations, frequency and amplitude

Sound begins as a vibration travelling through a medium such as air. A microphone converts changes in sound pressure into an electrical signal. In digital capture, an analogue-to-digital converter (ADC) measures that signal and represents it using numbers.

Frequency is the number of vibration cycles per second, measured in hertz (Hz). A higher frequency generally corresponds to a higher perceived pitch. Human hearing varies, but a frequently cited nominal range for young listeners is approximately 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz; it is not a guarantee for every listener or every playback device.

Amplitude refers to the size of signal variation. Greater amplitude often sounds louder, but perceived loudness also depends on frequency, duration and listening conditions. A waveform plots amplitude against time. Do not confuse waveform height with sound quality: a loud waveform can contain unwanted noise or distortion.

4.4 Sampling, sample rate and bit depth

Digital audio records a sequence of measurements called samples. The sample rate counts how many samples are captured each second per channel. Common rates include 44,100 samples/s (44.1 kHz) and 48,000 samples/s (48 kHz). A 48 kHz mono file contains 48,000 sample values each second.

The sampling theorem says that a band-limited signal requires a sample rate greater than twice its highest frequency to reconstruct it correctly under ideal conditions. Real capture systems use anti-aliasing filters. Merely increasing sample rate after recording does not recover frequencies that were never captured.

Bit depth describes the number of binary bits representing each uncompressed PCM sample, such as 16-bit or 24-bit. More bit depth provides a wider possible digital dynamic range and can make recording-level management more forgiving, but it does not fix a poor microphone or noisy room. Distinguish bit depth from bitrate, which describes the average number of encoded bits delivered per second for a file or stream.

Channels describe independent signals. Mono has one channel. Stereo normally has two channels. Mono is appropriate for many spoken-word guides; stereo may preserve useful directional or environmental context. A file converted from mono to dual-channel stereo does not magically gain new spatial information.

4.5 Worked calculation: uncompressed audio file size

For uncompressed PCM sample data, approximate bytes = sample rate × seconds × bit depth ÷ 8 × channels. For example, a 60-second mono 48 kHz, 24-bit PCM recording uses 48,000 × 60 × 24 ÷ 8 × 1 = 8,640,000 sample bytes, approximately 8.24 MiB (mebibytes). Container headers and metadata add some overhead.

A 60-second 48 kHz, 16-bit stereo recording uses 48,000 × 60 × 16 ÷ 8 × 2 = 11,520,000 sample bytes, approximately 10.99 MiB. The increase in channel count offsets the decrease in bit depth in this example.

A 96 kilobit/s MP3 lasting 60 seconds carries roughly 96,000 × 60 ÷ 8 = 720,000 bytes (about 0.69 MiB) of encoded data before file overhead and other effects. MP3 is lossy; its smaller size is not equivalent to the same captured information being retained.

4.6 Audio equipment and recording environments

A basic audio chain is source → microphone → preamplifier/interface → analogue-to-digital converter → file recorder → monitored playback. A smartphone can incorporate most of these components. A dedicated recorder or USB microphone may offer more control, predictable gain, and appropriate connections.

Directional microphones prioritise sound from some directions; omnidirectional microphones respond more evenly around the capsule. Microphone selection should consider the speaker count, room size, environmental noise and whether movement is expected. Place the microphone where speech is clear without invading personal space or handling fragile material.

Before recording, check the input device, settings, available storage and power; record ten seconds of test speech; listen on headphones; and confirm that peaks are not clipping. Reduce fans, traffic and reverberation where practical. Record a little room tone where appropriate so noise cleanup decisions can be evaluated. Avoid automatic noise suppression that destroys important evidence when preservation is the goal.

Gain is the amplification applied to the incoming signal. Recording too low may make unwanted system noise prominent when the track is later boosted; recording too high may exceed the digital ceiling and clip. Digital clipping is distortion caused when a signal exceeds the representable range. Once clipped on capture, the original waveform cannot be reconstructed exactly by lowering the volume afterwards.

4.7 Audio files: WAV, FLAC, MP3, AAC and Ogg

WAV is a widely supported container commonly holding uncompressed linear PCM audio. It is often used for production and preservation workflows, subject to institutional specifications. WAV can also contain other encodings, so inspect the codec and sample properties rather than assuming every WAV is uncompressed.

FLAC is lossless audio compression: it reduces storage while allowing exact reconstruction of the original decoded PCM data. Whether it is suitable as a managed preservation object depends on organisational policy and interoperability requirements. MP3 and AAC are common lossy codecs used for distribution and streaming; smaller files can support mobile access but audio data are discarded.

Ogg is a container that can carry different codecs, including Vorbis or Opus. File extensions are not complete technical descriptions. Before publication, check browser compatibility, permitted formats, quality at intended listening equipment, and whether a portable text alternative is supplied.

A sound preservation master and a web delivery copy serve different purposes. For a fictional interview, a controlled full-length 48 kHz/24-bit PCM master and a separately labelled compressed access derivative may be sensible. No one format guarantees authenticity, good recording quality or long-term preservation by itself.

4.8 Audio-editing concepts and a responsible workflow

Trimming removes unwanted material at the beginning or end; splitting separates sections into clips; fade-in/fade-out softens transitions; amplification changes signal level. Normalisation adjusts gain to reach a specified peak or loudness target. Peak normalisation and perceived-loudness normalisation are different operations: matching peaks does not guarantee identical subjective loudness.

Noise reduction can attenuate relatively steady hiss or hum, but excessive filtering may produce metallic artefacts and erase weak speech. In Audacity, a typical noise-reduction workflow uses a section containing noise alone to estimate a noise profile before processing the recording. Noise cleanup should improve intelligibility without fabricating words or concealing material edits.

Work on a copy. Import a source file, listen to the full recording, document its duration and format, make minimal justified edits, export a derivative with a new filename, and listen to the export from start to finish. For preservation-oriented material, retain the full unaltered source and maintain a log of changes and versions.

For a public orientation guide, retakes or removal of failed readings may be appropriate, provided the published content remains accurate. For evidential meeting recordings or oral-history holdings, removing a pause or word can change context. Make any edited listening version visibly distinct from the full managed source; do not present a re-edited derivative as an untouched original.

4.9 Audacity: a step-by-step first session

Install Audacity from an authorised source or use an approved audio editor. Connect the microphone, choose the appropriate input device, set the project sample rate when applicable and perform a test recording. Import the demonstration WAV if recording is not possible in class.

Observe the waveform, play the recording once, and identify silent sections, noise and clipping. Use the selection tool to trim a deliberately inserted long silence only in a copy. Avoid deleting meaningful pauses in an interview. Apply a modest fade where a cut creates a harsh edge. If a steady noise is present, try noise reduction on a duplicate and compare the before/after audio.

Export an access copy in MP3 or another suitable format with a meaningful name, e.g. U4-guide_access.mp3. Record the source filename, input and output settings, editing actions, editor/software and date. Listen to the exported file separately; a successful save operation does not prove clear or complete audio.

If no microphone is available, compare the provided synthetic WAV and MP3 files. The goal is not to guess which one sounds “best” blindly, but to identify file sizes, codec choices, duration, perceptible differences and plausible uses.

4.10 Transcription, speaker labels and multilingual audio

A transcript represents the meaningful spoken words and relevant non-speech sounds in text. It enables searching, translation, quotation, silent access and use by learners who cannot hear or prefer reading. A transcript should identify speakers where known and distinguish uncertain words rather than silently inventing a name or date.

Automated speech recognition (ASR) may save time but can make errors with names, technical terms, dialects, code-switching and background noise. A Kiswahili–English interview in Tanzania may include local place names, abbreviations and unfamiliar accents. Verify transcripts against the audio, keep the original language where relevant, and mark unintelligible sections honestly.

For example, a fictional recording might contain “Tuliweka kumbukumbu kwenye maktaba mwaka 1998.” If an ASR result changes the year to 1988, a search or historical interpretation can become misleading. Correction is an intellectual and quality-control task, not merely cosmetic text editing.

For audio-only media, W3C accessibility guidance treats a transcript as the essential text alternative for prerecorded spoken material under WCAG. Captions are primarily associated with synchronised media such as video; do not claim that separate video-style captions are automatically required for every standalone podcast.

4.11 Metadata, rights, consent and access controls

At minimum, describe title, identifier, collection/context, creation or recording date, language, duration, recording format, responsible party, speaker(s) when publication is permitted, rights/access conditions and relationship between source and derivative. Record technical details such as codec, sample rate, bit depth and channel count as needed.

Permission to record is not always the same as permission to publish, and consent may not replace other institutional or legal requirements. Explain the purpose and intended audience; document permission appropriately; respect withdrawal or restrictions according to the agreed procedure; and do not put sensitive interviews on public websites or unapproved AI-transcription services.

For a library podcast, check rights to scripts, music and any included excerpts. For an archives interview, distinguish interviewee, interviewer, date, collection provenance, source recording, transcripts, edits and access restrictions. If a voice might expose a vulnerable person, restrict playback and metadata rather than assuming that providing a transcript makes publication safe.

4.12 Digital audio preservation and integrity

Preservation is a managed process, not simply copying a file into a folder labelled archive. Keep a trusted source object, documented identifiers, appropriate format information, fixity checks where required, redundant backups across suitable locations, controlled access and a plan for storage or format changes.

A checksum such as SHA-256 can identify whether file bytes have changed between two checks; it does not by itself prove that a recording came from the claimed event or that the original recording was truthful. Authenticity depends on provenance and trustworthy custodial practices in addition to integrity checking.

For tapes and fragile legacy recordings, handling, equipment alignment and playback quality require specialist planning. Digitisation can introduce new errors; preserve documentation about the original medium and transfer process. IASA TC-04 is a widely recognised technical reference for production and preservation of digital audio objects.

4.13 BLISS and BRIM: two applied scenarios

BLISS example — A library produces a 90-second orientation guide for first-year students. The purpose is to explain how to find books and request assistance. A clear mono voice recording plus a checked transcript, playback controls and small MP3 access file can meet many student needs. Provide a descriptive title, duration, language, and text-first instructions for users with limited bandwidth.

BRIM example — A records office prepares a restricted-access oral-history sample for a curated exhibition. The full master includes its original pauses and recorded context; a derivative may contain a permitted extract clearly labelled as an edited excerpt. Attach date, speaker identifiers when authorised, consent terms, transcript version and the relationship to the master. Do not use actual confidential records in classroom demonstrations.

Both projects need quality checks: intelligible audio, playback on multiple devices, correct file naming, complete matching transcript, documented editing and permission to publish. The archival scenario adds particularly strong provenance and access considerations; the library scenario emphasises audience learning and ease of discovery.

Audience: new students. Outputs: concise narration, transcript, optional MP3 access copy, descriptive webpage and brief usability check.

Audience: authorised researchers. Outputs: full controlled source, accurately described derivative, reviewed transcript, provenance and consent/access documentation.

4.14 A small quality assurance checklist

Before submission, listen to the first, middle and end of the exported file and ideally the whole audio; confirm that there is no unintended missing material, abrupt start, excessive hiss, clipping or misleading edit. Check duration and playback speed, inspect whether channels are silent or unbalanced, and compare source and derivative properties.

Compare the transcript line by line with the audio, including names, numbers and Kiswahili words. Indicate uncertain words. Ensure that title, identifier, speaker or narrator role, date and access rights are understandable. Ask a classmate to perform the intended user task without receiving special instructions. Revise the access copy or description if the listener is confused.

Remember that the illustrative MP3/WAV file-size comparison is not a universal sound-quality contest. The recording method, microphone placement, bitrates, codec, content, listening conditions and purpose all matter.

4.15 Case walkthrough: a complete audio guide from idea to release

Imagine that a library wants a short recorded explanation of how students can locate a book and request help. First, define the user task: a new student should know where to begin, what information they need and whom to contact. Write a script with plain-language steps. If the guide uses institutional names or opening hours, check them with the responsible service before recording; do not invent details.

Next, plan the recording. A single narrator will speak for roughly 45–60 seconds in a reasonably quiet room. Use a suitable microphone, a test recording and monitored levels; do not aim for a distorted, overly loud waveform. Save the original file with an identifier such as U4-LIB-guide_source.wav. Record its format and duration in a manifest. Make a working copy, remove an accidental false start only when the intended final guide genuinely requires it, and export a separate U4-LIB-guide_access.mp3. The sample source and access files in this unit demonstrate these distinct roles.

Prepare a full checked transcript. Write a page title such as “Library orientation audio guide”, note the language and approximate duration, and include a text-first equivalent of the directions. Publish with an audio player that requires the user to press Play. Test the task: ask a classmate unfamiliar with the guide to explain the first step. Check the transcript, mobile data cost, playback and the institution's permission to publish. This workflow yields a useful learning resource, not merely an audio file.

4.16 Case walkthrough: preserving interview context

Imagine a fictional recorded interview about records kept by an older government office. The interviewee pauses before giving a date and corrects themselves: “It was 1997 — no, 1998.” An editor who removes the correction to make the recording sound smoother could create a misleading record of what was said. A responsible archives workflow keeps the complete source and accurately describes any curated excerpt. A transcript should preserve the correction, or explicitly show an editorial convention if an adjusted reading version is produced.

Suppose a request comes to publish a 20-second excerpt. Check the recorded person's publication permission and any restrictions; record the source identifier, excerpt start and end times, editing operations and new derivative identifier. If publication is not permitted, create a synthetic demonstration instead. An access file's copyright status and privacy conditions do not follow solely from its file format or compression. An MP3 can be restricted, and a WAV can be freely shareable if rights allow.

4.17 Timestamps, speakers and uncertainty in transcripts

For longer recordings, a transcript may benefit from time references and speaker labels. A simple convention could be [00:35] Interviewer: and [00:38] Participant:. Where the speaker's identity cannot be published, use an approved generic label. A timestamp refers to the recording version being described; if an access excerpt has been cut, its times may differ from those in the full source, so provide the relationship clearly.

Use consistent editorial conventions for unintelligible words, uncertain spellings, audible laughter or extended pauses. Distinguish verbatim transcription, which closely records what was spoken, from an edited transcript, which may improve grammar or readability. Either may have a legitimate purpose, but the reader should know which version they are seeing. A translation into another language is an additional interpretation rather than a replacement for the source language.

4.18 Low-bandwidth and responsible publication choices

Some students use mobile data and older devices. Offer a compressed access format that works on the target devices, display the file's approximate size and duration, and avoid automatic playback. A readable transcript can sometimes supply the same informational content more quickly. Never assume that every user wants audio simply because it is available. Provide descriptive link text, sufficient controls and keyboard access through standard HTML audio elements.

Where a recording may contain sensitive identifiers, do not embed it in a public page merely to test web playback. Check whether the audio itself, transcript, filename and metadata reveal confidential information. Public samples should be fictional or expressly cleared; the exercise files supplied with this course are synthetic. Sound editing applications and online services differ in their data practices, so follow the institution's approved tools and policy before uploading real interviews.
