LIU08106 / RIU08106 · Unit 04

Review Questions and Applied Exercises

Sound science, file choices, editing practice, transcription and professional judgement.

← Back to Unit 4 on the course site

Part A — Understanding sound and digital audio

  1. Explain how sound travels from a person speaking to a stored digital audio file.
  2. Distinguish frequency, pitch, amplitude and loudness. Give a practical example of each.
  3. Describe the meaning of waveform and give two limits of interpreting audio by its waveform alone.
  4. Distinguish analogue recording, analogue-to-digital conversion and digital storage.
  5. Explain sampling rate and bit depth, including why increasing either after recording cannot recreate missing signal detail.
  6. What is digital clipping? Suggest three preventive actions while recording.
  7. Compare mono and stereo for a 60-second library voice guide and a two-speaker environmental recording.

Part B — Formats, compression and calculations

  1. Compare PCM WAV, FLAC, MP3 and AAC with respect to coding, storage and typical use.
  2. Distinguish lossless compression from lossy compression using sound-file examples.
  3. Explain why a WAV extension alone cannot confirm a file is uncompressed PCM.
  4. Calculate sample payload bytes and MiB for 45 seconds of 48 kHz, 24-bit, mono PCM. Show every step.
  5. Calculate sample payload bytes for a 90-second, 44.1 kHz, 16-bit stereo file.
  6. Estimate the data payload in bytes for a 120-second MP3 at 128 kb/s, ignoring overhead.
  7. Explain why a compact MP3 should normally remain separate from a controlled archival master.

Part C — Recording, processing and evaluation

  1. Develop a six-point pre-recording checklist for a student podcast in a noisy campus building.
  2. Differentiate trimming, splitting, amplification, peak normalisation and noise reduction.
  3. Why can excessive noise reduction damage oral-history evidence?
  4. Write a step-by-step workflow to edit a 45-second library orientation recording in Audacity.
  5. What is an edit log? Draft five fields and explain why each is useful.
  6. Describe an effective quality-control test that can detect missing audio after export.

Part D — Transcription, accessibility and ethics

  1. Distinguish a human-verified transcript from an automatic speech-recognition draft.
  2. Why might a Kiswahili-English interview be difficult for automated transcription? Suggest four checks.
  3. Give an example of how a misheard year can affect information retrieval or historical interpretation.
  4. Explain the importance of a transcript for prerecorded audio-only material.
  5. Distinguish consent to record from permission to publish or reuse a recording.
  6. Explain why an audio checksum does not establish the truthful historical origin of a recording.

Part E — Professional applications

  1. BLISS: Propose a 90-second library orientation audio package with format, transcript, metadata and user test.
  2. BRIM: Plan an oral-history excerpt including master protection, provenance, transcript and access restrictions.
  3. Compare the professional editing constraints of an institutional promotional guide and a recorded council meeting.
  4. Write a short metadata record for a synthetic interview including title, identifier, language, date, duration and derivative relationship.
  5. Design a simple user-centred evaluation of an audio guide for students who access materials on low-bandwidth mobile connections.

Worked answer support

Audio storage calculations

PCM sample bytes = sample rate × duration in seconds × bit depth ÷ 8 × channel count. MiB = bytes ÷ 1,048,576. Compressed bitrate estimate = bitrate in bits/s × seconds ÷ 8; actual encoded file size can differ.

What makes a strong applied answer?

Identify the user or purpose; justify media and format decisions; specify source/master and access versions; explain permission and confidentiality; require a checked transcript and a measurable quality test.

How should I discuss editing?

Explain the original problem, the operation performed, its effect on clarity, the risk of altering meaning, and how the original source and processing history remain available.
