Multimedia Authoring, Hypermedia and Interaction

Design a multimedia resource that users can understand, navigate, test and trust.

← Back to Unit 7 on the course site

Start here: a familiar situation

Imagine a folder containing photographs, recordings and descriptions of an exhibition. A visitor cannot tell where to begin or how one item relates to another. A multimedia guide adds a home screen, meaningful links, captions, navigation and feedback so visitors can complete a task. The design must also work with a keyboard, on a phone and for people who need alternatives to images or sound.

Key words in simple language

  • Authoring: combining media and controls into a usable information experience.
  • Hypertext: linked pieces of text or information.
  • Hypermedia: linked content that can include text, images, audio and video.
  • Sitemap: a diagram of pages or screens and their relationships.

Think about it: Why is uploading five unrelated files not the same as publishing an interactive guide? The sections below explain how to answer this question.

For other terms, see the course multimedia glossary.

Learning outcomes. By the end of this unit, explain authoring and hypermedia; design a sitemap and storyboard; compare authoring tools; prototype navigation; apply usability, accessibility, rights and provenance checks; and evaluate a BLISS or BRIM interactive guide.

7.1 Multimedia authoring and linked information

From media files to an authored experience

A folder of image, audio, video and text files is a collection of assets, not automatically a coherent multimedia experience.

Authoring gives assets structure: screens, layout, messages, navigation controls, sequencing, feedback and links.

For example, a visitor chooses a historical period, opens a photograph, reads its description and returns to the timeline.

Consider an oral-history collection. Audio playback, a transcript, the interview date, a subject index and a link to related records combine to create a service. They must be arranged deliberately rather than posted as unrelated files.

Key idea: Structure and user choices transform files into an information service.

What multimedia authoring means

Multimedia authoring is the planning, integration and arrangement of multiple media elements into a publishable learning or information resource.

An authoring environment may provide page layouts, timelines, templates, links, hotspots, actions, preview and export.

Authoring is different from editing a single photo or video: it defines how the elements work together for a user.

A slide editor, content-management tool and a web authoring framework all support different output types. What matters is what they allow students or visitors to do—and what the institution can continue maintaining.

Key idea: An authored system combines content, relationships, behaviour and presentation.

Hypertext and hypermedia

Hypertext connects textual information using links; a user follows references between documents or sections.

Hypermedia extends linked navigation across images, audio, video, maps, text and interactive objects.

Example: selecting a map region opens an archival photograph, an oral-history excerpt and contextual catalogue data.

A link may connect a term to its definition, a place on a map to a photograph, or an audio clip to a transcript. Connections should advance understanding rather than simply decorate a page.

Key idea: Hypermedia is about linked resources and user-driven movement, not just animated pages.

Linear, non-linear and mixed structures

Linear: fixed order, such as a narrated training video or compulsory safety induction.

Non-linear: users choose among locations, themes or topics; a hypermedia exhibition has multiple possible paths.

Mixed: a guided introduction leads to freely browsable sections, with clear back and home controls throughout.

For a guided training video, fixed sequencing can prevent a learner from missing a prerequisite. For exploratory collections, forced sequencing can conceal relevant records. A mixed structure often works well for beginners.

Key idea: Choose structure according to learning goals and the freedom users require.

Nodes, links, anchors and destinations

A node is a page, screen, section or media item. A link connects a source node to a target node.

The anchor is the clickable or focusable label, image region or button that exposes the link; its text should communicate purpose.

A broken link points nowhere. An orphan page has no discoverable inbound route. Both frustrate access and quality control.

Draw each page as a box and each navigation action as an arrow. Check whether every page is discoverable starting from the home screen. Reaching a dead end is a problem unless it is explicitly a final step with a clear exit.

Key idea: Draw navigation as a graph before building screens.

7.2 Information architecture, maps and journeys

Information architecture and content grouping

Information architecture organises content into meaningful categories, labels, hierarchies and paths for finding information.

Begin with user questions: “Where are the opening hours?”, “How do I access an archival record?”, “What am I looking at?”

Group related items, use consistent terminology, and avoid making one page a dumping ground for every asset.

Labels should reflect users’ vocabulary. For instance, a new student usually understands “Borrow a book” faster than “Circulation services”. Specialists may prefer domain-specific labels where needed.

Key idea: Good navigation starts with clear content organisation.

A practical sitemap: digital library guide

Home → Search the catalogue / Borrow resources / Research support / Contact the library.

Each branch explains a single task and contains a relevant example, a next step and a route back to Home.

A student should reach the catalogue demonstration in one or two intentional decisions, not through guesswork.

Use consistent section naming in all menus and headings. “Search catalogue” should not unexpectedly become “Resource locator” on the following screen.

Key idea: Write labels based on actual tasks, not vague words like “More” or “Click here”.

A practical sitemap: archival exhibition

Home → Time periods → Featured items → Item detail → Rights and source information.

At each item include a title, date/uncertainty, provenance, contextual description, permitted media and a return path.

Use only fictional or cleared material in class; archival access must follow permissions and sensitivity review.

For archives, context and restrictions need to appear near the item, not hidden in a document that visitors never open. Approximate dates should be indicated as approximate.

Key idea: An attractive exhibit must not erase context, provenance or restrictions.

Storyboards and screen-by-screen planning

A storyboard is a numbered plan of screens or scenes indicating what appears, what the user does and what happens next.

A useful screen row contains screen ID, goal, heading, media, interaction, next/back destination and accessibility notes.

The storyboard helps identify missing transitions and unnecessary media before software implementation.

Storyboarding before implementation reduces rework. If the plan shows a return button without a destination, it has exposed a problem that is cheaper to fix on paper than after publication.

Key idea: Storyboard behaviour as well as appearance.

User journeys: start, choice, result, recovery

A user journey describes a specific goal from entry to successful completion, including likely detours and errors.

Example: a new student opens the library guide, chooses “Search”, follows the catalogue demonstration and finds the help option.

Provide recovery paths: Home, Back, clear labels, helpful empty states and no irreversible surprise actions.

Write one realistic task: “Starting from the home page, find a tutorial on searching for journal articles.” Count steps, note hesitations and ensure help is available.

Key idea: Test whether a real student can complete the intended task.

7.3 Authoring tools, web structure and interactions

Multimedia authoring tools: selecting a category

Presentation tools can build linear tutorials and hyperlink-based guides; visual website builders can publish simple structured pages.

HTML/CSS/JavaScript can provide flexible offline web interaction; learning-authoring tools can offer branching and quizzes.

Compare output portability, offline use, accessibility controls, cost, media handling and long-term maintenance—not just templates.

A tool checklist should include cost, supported file formats, accessibility options, whether work can be exported to open files, whether it works offline and whether data is uploaded to a third-party service.

Key idea: The best authoring tool fits the audience, output and institution.

Static web authoring is practical for this course

A static website can combine HTML structure, CSS styling, JavaScript interactions, images, video, captions and downloads.

A simple guide can work offline from a folder and later be published on GitHub Pages, without a database or user accounts.

Static does not mean non-interactive; however, local progress is not a formal submission, and restricted material should not be published.

Static webpages are especially useful for classroom practice because they can be stored in a folder and published without a live server-side application. They still need proper testing and safe content governance.

Key idea: A small static site is suitable for safe prototypes and general information.

The three roles of HTML, CSS and JavaScript

HTML gives semantic structure: headings, navigation, links, lists, forms, captions and media controls.

CSS controls appearance, spacing, contrast, responsive layout and visible focus states.

JavaScript manages states such as chosen exhibit item, question feedback or a branching flow; core information should remain accessible.

Use headings to reflect document sections, not simply to make letters appear large. A script may show a new panel without changing pages; the meaning of that panel still needs an understandable label.

Key idea: Separate meaning, presentation and interaction to simplify maintenance.

Buttons, links and hotspots: use them correctly

Use a link when the action navigates to another page or a named location. Use a button when an action changes the interface.

A hotspot should have a meaningful accessible name and a keyboard-operable equivalent; avoid pointer-only invisible areas.

Choose labels such as “View 1960s photographs” instead of “Click here”; identify downloads and external destinations.

Links and buttons have different native behaviour. A keyboard user can follow an anchor link using Enter; a real button supports keyboard activation and produces an action when properly connected.

Key idea: Make actions predictable and operable without a mouse.

Feedback, controls and system status

A user should know which screen or item is selected, which action just occurred and what to do next.

Provide concise feedback: “2 of 4 questions correct”, “Item 3 of 5”, “No results for that term”, or “Download starts”.

Avoid hidden automatic playback, surprise navigation, inaccessible drag-only controls and misleading success messages.

When a quiz is graded, a result such as “4 of 5 correct” is more informative than a green tick alone. Errors should explain how the user can recover.

Key idea: Visibility and predictable feedback reduce user confusion.

7.4 Usability and accessibility by design

Usability: usefulness, ease and efficiency

Usability asks whether specified users achieve specified goals effectively and efficiently, with satisfaction in context.

Assess task completion, navigation errors, unnecessary steps, comprehension, frustration and recovery—not beauty alone.

A quick five-task test may reveal serious problems even when the pages look professionally designed.

Usability evidence can be simple. Give a participant three tasks, note whether they succeeded without a hint, and ask what was confusing. Avoid directing them during the test.

Key idea: Observe tasks and outcomes instead of relying only on opinions.

Accessibility: why it matters

Users may rely on keyboards, screen readers, magnification, captions or colour-independent cues.

Design for clear headings, readable contrast, descriptive alternatives, keyboard operability and a logical focus sequence.

WCAG 2.2 provides testable criteria; a small checklist helps, but cannot establish full WCAG conformance.

Accessibility decisions are made throughout planning. Selecting text labels and manageable media sizes early is easier than retrofitting every screen after development.

Key idea: Accessibility is part of authoring and testing, not decoration.

Semantic structure and readable navigation

Provide a descriptive page title, one primary heading, ordered subheadings, a labelled navigation region and meaningful link text.

Use real headings and lists rather than a long series of visually styled generic blocks.

Make current location identifiable through text or a clear selected state, not colour alone.

A screen-reader user benefits when navigation is marked as navigation and headings follow a meaningful sequence. These semantic choices carry information even without visual CSS.

Key idea: Semantic structure supports comprehension and assistive technology.

Keyboard operation, focus and target size

A keyboard user should move through interactive controls with Tab/Shift+Tab and activate expected actions with Enter or Space.

A visible focus indicator shows which control is active. Avoid trapping focus in a custom exhibit or menu.

Make touch targets usable on mobile; test both large and small screens, and try every essential interaction without a mouse.

To simulate keyboard use, put the mouse aside. Start from the address bar, press Tab through the site, watch where focus moves and activate controls without clicking. If a required control cannot be used, redesign it.

Key idea: Never make the only route depend on hovering or dragging.

Contrast, typography and responsive presentation

WCAG 2.2 AA normal-text contrast is generally at least 4.5:1; large text generally at least 3:1, with stated exceptions.

Use readable type sizes, sufficient spacing, sensible line lengths and a layout that reflows on narrow screens.

Colour should not be the sole signal of status; pair it with text, icons or other explicit information.

Contrast ratios are measured relationships between foreground and background colours. The general WCAG AA normal text rule is 4.5:1, while qualifying large text has a 3:1 rule; special cases exist.

Key idea: A design is not readable merely because it looks attractive on one monitor.

Alternative text and complex media

Meaningful images need concise alternative text that communicates the same essential purpose as the image in its context.

Decorative graphics should not produce noisy descriptions; complex archival charts may need longer adjacent descriptions.

Video needs appropriate captions, transcripts and, where needed, description of important visual-only information.

Write alternatives for meaningful content: “Map showing three library branches around Arusha, with the main branch on Njiro Road” is more useful than “image”. For decorative shapes, empty alt text may be appropriate.

Key idea: Provide equivalent information for users who cannot use the original media channel.

7.5 Rights, provenance and quality control

Ethics, copyright and rights notices

An author can only use media they created, received permission to use, or can otherwise lawfully include under applicable rules.

Archive collections may require contextual warnings, privacy screening and explicit reproduction or access restrictions.

Record source, rights basis and attribution; an interactive interface does not automatically clear copyright.

Permission to digitise an item is not necessarily permission to publish it worldwide. Check licences, individual consent, personal information and any institutional access restrictions before release.

Key idea: Rights and privacy screening are release gates, not optional captions.

Metadata, provenance and content integrity

For each asset record title/identifier, creator or source, date/uncertainty, rights, format, description and its screen relationship.

Use stable identifiers and readable filenames so linked assets can be updated without losing track of context.

Do not present a transformed image or shortened recording as an untouched original; label interpretations and derivatives.

Identify the creator and the origin of a digital item. A photograph’s file name or visual appearance alone is not enough to establish when it was taken or who created it.

Key idea: A well-authored system lets users understand where content came from.

Testing navigation with a graph

List every screen (node) and every intended link (directed edge); verify that each destination actually exists.

Test reachability from Home: if an item can only be opened using a hidden URL, it is effectively an orphan for normal users.

Cycles are not automatically errors—Home → Gallery → Home is useful; dead ends and unclear loops are problems.

An automated list of pages and destinations catches missing files and some structural errors. It cannot establish whether labels make sense or whether linked content is appropriate.

Key idea: A sitemap is also a systematic link-testing tool.

7.6 Profession-specific examples and evaluation

Worked example: five-screen library orientation

Screens: Home, Search, Borrow, Help and Credits. Home offers Search/Borrow; every task screen offers Help and Home.

User task: start at Home, learn a catalogue search step, reach Help, return Home. Capture whether actions are predictable.

Include a short captioned or textual tutorial, clear labels and an attribution block for any media.

A five-page orientation is large enough to demonstrate navigation and small enough for a beginner to test. Ensure that each screen does a specific job and avoid filler pages.

Key idea: A small tested guide is better than an ambitious broken prototype.

Worked example: five-screen archival showcase

Screens: Home, Periods, Item, Context and Rights. The item page links to Context and Rights and back to Periods.

Display a fictional photograph with title, approximate date, description, and a flag that it is a teaching simulation.

Test that restrictions are visible before access and that context is not separated from the displayed item.

Archival interpretation should distinguish evidence from explanation. A narrative about a period is not the same as a record created during that period; both can appear but should be labelled correctly.

Key idea: Hypermedia should deepen historical understanding, not just produce clicks.

Evaluate an interactive prototype

Recruit a peer to complete three tasks without assistance; note task success, time/steps and any hesitation or incorrect paths.

Then test keyboard-only navigation, readable zoom, descriptive labels, mobile layout, contrast and available alternatives.

Record defects with severity, reproduction steps, fixes and a re-test result; avoid claiming full accessibility certification.

Usability testing and technical verification complement each other. An otherwise valid link is still poor when users do not understand its purpose.

Key idea: Document evidence of user success and repairs.

A practical authoring workflow

1. Specify users and goals → 2. Audit approved assets → 3. Draw sitemap and user journey → 4. Storyboard screens.

5. Author interface and link assets → 6. Check rights and metadata → 7. Test task flow and accessibility → 8. Publish an approved copy.

Archive the source project, final web package, manifest, feedback and change history separately as needed.

Keep a revision log with issue, impact, fix and retest. A reproducible project can be improved by another member of the team.

Key idea: Make the production process repeatable and reviewable.

7.7 Implementation examples and delivery

Simple HTML link and button examples

Navigation: <a href="guide.html">Open library guide</a> creates a destination link with meaningful anchor text.

Action: <button type="button">Show item details</button> changes content when wired to a real event handler.

Do not make a clickable <div> without keyboard semantics. Ensure buttons operate correctly and dynamic feedback is announced.

Students with basic HTML knowledge can create a self-contained mini-site. Those using PowerPoint with hyperlinks must still check navigation and export behaviour.

Key idea: Use the right built-in HTML element before adding complicated scripting.

Plan a low-bandwidth and offline experience

Keep essential descriptions in text, resize access images appropriately and avoid unnecessary autoplay or huge background video.

Include captions/transcripts and clear downloads so users can choose a lower-data path.

Prefer relative links for offline and GitHub Pages portability; test after moving the folder and on a phone.

Images, sound and video should improve user tasks instead of slowing them down. Text alternatives allow a person to make progress even when media fails to load.

Key idea: Reliable delivery is part of usable multimedia authoring.

Guided class practical

Build a five-screen prototype using fictional or cleared assets, a simple sitemap, logical labels and working navigation.

Use the supplied planning template, test the complete path from Home to a relevant item, and report any broken/orphan link.

Have a peer attempt one BLISS/BRIM task keyboard-only; record failures and a specific improvement.

A lightweight static guide can be packed as a ZIP and opened locally. Before publishing to a public repository, confirm there are no credentials, student grades or restricted records.

Key idea: Evidence matters: submit the working prototype and a concise test report.

7.8 Detailed case study: designing for students

BLISS practical application

First-year library orientation

Imagine a new student who has never used the institutional catalogue. The goal is to locate learning material, see an understandable search example and learn where to ask for assistance. Develop five screens named Home, Search, Borrow, Help and Credits. Home links to Search and Borrow; task pages offer Help and Home. Present a simple screenshot or invented illustration labelled as a demonstration, an accessible text explanation and a rights note. Give the prototype to a colleague, ask them to locate the search instructions and record whether the task was completed without a hint.

BRIM practical application

Fictional archives mini-exhibition

Imagine an archives visitor exploring a simulated collection of local-government records. The goal is to identify a fictional item, read the interpretation and understand its source and access restrictions. Develop Home, Timeline, Item, Context and Rights screens. Provide a discoverable route to Rights and Context from the item view. State clearly that records are fictional teaching materials. Never copy or publish confidential real records for this exercise. Test whether users can identify the period, locate an item and return safely to Home.

7.9 Worked checklist for release

  1. Define the intended user, goal and content boundaries.
  2. Review every asset for authorisation, description and attribution.
  3. Prepare the sitemap and storyboard and check all intended destinations.
  4. Build a working prototype, with real buttons and links rather than purely decorative controls.
  5. Test navigation from the entry screen, including recovery from wrong paths.
  6. Test with a keyboard, captions or transcript where relevant, readable type and narrow screens.
  7. Ask a peer to complete a realistic task and record a concrete usability issue.
  8. Repair and retest; package the source files, manifest and an honest limitations note.

7.10 Practical work, assignment and revision

Professional assignment: choose a path

BLISS: design a navigable digital library orientation with searchable resource guidance and help information.

BRIM: design a fictional archives mini-exhibition with contextual item descriptions, rights statement and source-to-item links.

Both must include a sitemap, storyboard, accessible controls, a testing log, rights/attribution and a short reflection.

A guided practical should produce evidence—not just a screenshot. A sitemap, working links, testing record and attribution explain what the prototype achieves and where it still needs work.

Key idea: Different professional settings, shared principles of usable design.

Common errors and their corrections

Broken navigation: give every button an action and every link a valid destination; inspect path and filename case.

Inaccessible controls: test keyboard focus and activation, labelled fields, image alternatives and appropriate contrast.

Misleading content: display context, rights and restrictions rather than placing them in an undiscoverable footer.

Different subject applications can use the same marking criteria. Both prototypes must make the source, audience and permitted access clear.

Key idea: Revise for functionality, clarity and trust before adding more effects.

Summary: designing connected information experiences

Authoring links text, images, sound and video into a structured, interactive resource that serves user goals.

A good prototype combines information architecture, meaningful navigation, consistent design, accessibility and ethical media use.

Next: Digitisation Planning and Quality Control, where the reliability of original captured media becomes the centre of attention.

Revision is successful when a learner can justify the interface design, follow its paths and explain how errors and accessibility needs were addressed.

Key idea: Author for real users; verify navigation and make content understandable.
